14          On April 30, 2026, this Court found that Petitioner’s habeas petition (Dkt. 1) and traverse

15   (Dkt. 10) contained citation errors which appeared to be “the result of using generative artificial

16   intelligence without checking the citations generated by the program.” Dkt. 12 at 3. The Court

17   ordered Petitioner’s counsel to show cause as to why they should not be sanctioned pursuant to

18   Rule 11. Id. at 3–4. Within 24 hours, Julian Mansoor Daman—counsel responsible for drafting

19   Petitioner’s briefs—filed a declaration explaining the origin of the errant citations. Dkt. 13.

21          Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 11 “imposes a duty on attorneys to certify that they have

22   conducted a reasonable inquiry and have determined that any papers filed with the court are well

23   grounded in fact, legally tenable, and ‘not interposed for any improper purpose.’” Cooter & Gell

24   v. Hartmarx Corp., 496 U.S. 384, 393 (1990). Rule 11 provides, in relevant part, that:

            formed after an inquiry reasonable under the circumstances: . . .

            (2) the claims, defenses, and other legal contentions are warranted by existing law
 4          or by a nonfrivolous argument for extending, modifying, or reversing existing law
            or for establishing new law.

     Fed. R. Civ. P. 11(b)(2). “On its own, the court may order an attorney, law firm, or party to show

     P. 11(c)(3).

            Courts have “wide discretion in determining the appropriate sanction for a Rule 11
 9
     violation.” Hudson v. Moore Bus. Forms, Inc., 836 F.2d 1156, 1163 (9th Cir. 1987). “A sanction
10
     imposed under this rule must be limited to what suffices to deter repetition of the conduct or
11
     comparable conduct by others similarly situated.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 11(c)(4).

13

14

     detention exceeded 16 months); Nadarajah, 443 F.3d at 1080 (“[t]he nearly five-year detention
20
     in this case far exceeds both any period of confinement found reasonable by the Court, and the
21
     six-month period of presumptive reasonableness.”). The Court thus ordered Petitioner’s counsel

 5           Petitioner’s counsel responded to the Court’s order promptly, filing a declaration later

 6   that same day. Dkt. 13. Counsel averred that “a group of legal interns” assigned to research had

 7   used Google Gemini to generate text used in the filings. Id. ¶ 5. The program “fabricated the

 8   quotes and confidently presented this information as binding Ninth Circuit precedent.” Id. ¶ 6.

 9   Counsel further states that his interns “failed to mention this to [him], and have since been

10   released from [his] employ.” Id. Counsel apologized, accepting “full and complete responsibility

11   for the errors contained in these filings” and stated that his “critical failure under Rule 11 was

12   relying on [his] interns’ research without conducting his own independent verification of its

13   authenticity.” Id. ¶¶ 2, 7.

14           “The signing attorney cannot leave it to some trusted subordinate, or to one of his

15   partners, to satisfy himself that the filed paper is factually and legally responsible; by signing he

16   represents not merely the fact that it is so, but also the fact that he personally has applied his own

17   judgment.” Pavelic & LeFlore v. Marvel Ent. Grp., 493 U.S. 120, 125 (1989). By signing

18   Petitioner’s briefs, counsel—rather than his interns or the artificial intelligence program they

19   used—“confidently presented” fabricated quotes to this Court. “[I]t is of no consequence

20   whether [counsel] used AI to draft the brief because ‘[c]iting nonexistent case law or

21   misrepresenting the holdings of a case is making a false statement to a court,’ regardless of

22   whether generative AI suggested the content.” Oneto v. Watson, 808 F. Supp. 3d 974, 980 (N.D.

23   Cal. 2025) (quoting United States v. Hayes, 763 F. Supp. 3d 1054, 1067 (E.D. Cal. 2025)).

 1          “Rule 11 sanctions are meant to serve several purposes, including (1) deterring future

 2   litigation abuse, (2) punishing present litigation abuse, (3) compensating victims of litigation

 3   abuse, and (4) streamlining court dockets and facilitating case management.” Wadsworth v.

 4   Walmart Inc., 348 F.R.D. 489, 497 (D. Wyo. 2025) (quoting White v. Gen. Motors Corp., 908

 5   F.2d 675, 683 (10th Cir. 1990)). Sanctions for fabricated citations may include “(1) monetary

 6   sanctions, (2) striking the filing containing erroneous citations, (3) requiring written notification

 7   to the client, (4) requiring written notification to judges incorrectly identified as having authored

 8   nonexistent cases, and (5) referring attorneys to the appropriate disciplinary body for disciplinary

 9   proceedings.” Id. Courts addressing similar misconduct have issued fines between $1,000 and

10   $5,000. See id. (collecting cases).

11          The Court recognizes that “any sanction imposed must be proportionate to the offense

12   and commensurate with principles of restraint and dignity inherent in judicial power.” Id.

13   (quoting Zambrano v. City of Tustin, 885 F.2d 1473, 1480 (9th Cir. 1989)). It also recognizes

14   that several facts weigh against a severe sanction here, including that (1) counsel quickly filed a

15   declaration admitting fault and explaining the origin of the false citations; (2) there is no record

16   of counsel committing similar Rule 11 violations in the past; and (3) the errant citations were

17   disregarded by the Court and did not affect Petitioner’s habeas proceedings. See Dkt. 21 at 4 n.1.

18   Given these facts, the Court finds that a financial penalty of one thousand dollars ($1,000)

19   against the drafting attorney, along with this public reprimand, is sufficient to deter future abuse.

20          Petitioner’s other attorney, Jennifer Nimer, served as Washington local counsel only and

21   was not involved in the drafting of Plaintiffs’ briefs. Dkt. 13 ¶ 3; Dkt. 14 ¶ 2. But as an attorney

22   signing the brief, she too “had a nondelegable duty to ensure a motion or filing is supported by

23   existing law.” Wadsworth, 348 F.R.D. at 498–99; see also id. at 498 (discussing $2,500 fine “for

24   the local counsel for serving as a rubber stamp for attorney admitted pro hac vice”). The Court
