                                  15          Under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 30, a party may request the deposition of another

                                  16   party to the litigation by serving a notice upon that party. Fed. R. Civ. P. 30(b)(1). Federal Rule of

                                  18   Civil Procedure 45(d)(3), a district court “must quash or modify a subpoena that: (i) fails to allow a

                                  21   waiver applies; or (iv) subjects a person to undue burden.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 45(d)(3).

                                  23   order “to protect a party or person from annoyance, embarrassment, oppression, or undue burden or

                                  24   expense.” Fed. R. Civ. P 26(c). A protective order requires a showing that “particularized harm

                                  16           Pursuant to Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 30, a party may request the deposition of another

                                  17   party to the litigation by serving a notice upon that party. Fed. R. Civ. P. 30(b)(1). An employee

                                  18   or agent who qualifies as an “officer, director or managing agent” is subject to deposition by notice

                                  19   pursuant to Rule 30(b)(1). Cadent Ltd. v. 3M Unitek Corp., 232 F.R.D. 625, 627-28 & n.1 (C.D.

                                  20   Cal. 2005). Courts consider several factors in determining whether an individual is a managing

                                  21   agent under Rule 30(b)(1): “(1) the discretionary authority vested in the person by the corporation;

                                  22   (2) the employee’s dependability in following the employer’s directions; (3) whether the individual

                                  23   is more likely to identify with the corporation or the adverse party in the litigation; and (4) the degree

                                  24   of supervisory authority in areas pertinent to the litigation.” E.I. DuPont de Nemours & Co. v. Kolon

                                  25   Indus., Inc., 268 F.R.D. 45, 48-49 (E.D. Va. 2010) (citing In re Honda, Am. Motor Co., 168 F.R.D.

                                  26   535, 540-41 (D. Md. 1996)). Courts recognize that the third factor is “paramount.” Id. at 49 (quoting

                                  27   Honda, 168 F.R.D. at 541). “[T]he question of whether a particular person is a ‘managing agent’ is

                                  28   to be answered pragmatically, on an ad hoc basis, considering the facts of the particular case.”
                                                                                           5
                                         Case 3:25-cv-03698-SI         Document 395         Filed 05/13/26    Page 6 of 9




                                   1   Calderon v. Experian Info. Sols., Inc., 287 F.R.D. 629, 632 (D. Idaho 2012), aff’d, 290 F.R.D. 508

                                   2   (D. Idaho 2013); see also Founding Church of Scientology v. Webster, 802 F.2d 1448, 1452 (D.C.

                                   3   Cir. 1986). “[I]f there is at least a ‘close question’ as to the managing agent status of a potential

                                   4   witness, doubts should be resolved in favor of allowing the deposition[.]” Calderon, 287 F.R.D. at

                                   5   633.

                                   6          Generally the determination of an individual’s status as a “managing agent” is “determined

                                   7   as of the time of the deposition, not as of the time when the activities disputed in the litigation

                                   8   occurred.” E.I. DuPont, 268 F.R.D. at 49 (citation omitted). However, the general rule that “former

                                   9   employees cannot be managing agents of a corporation” is not without exceptions. Id. “[C]ourts

                                  10   have made exceptions to this general rule, for example when a corporation terminates an officer in

                                  11   light of pending litigation, plans to rehire the individual in another position, or an individual

                                  12   continues to act as a managing agent despite no longer being an employee.” In re Lithium Ion



 United States District Court
                                  13   Batteries Antitrust Litig., No. 13-MD-02420-YGR (DMR), 2015 WL 5440789, at *6 (N.D. Cal.

                                  14   Sept. 15, 2015) (quoting Rundquist v. Vapiano SE, 277 F.R.D. 205, 208 (D.D.C. 2011)) (citing

                                  15   Honda, 168 F.R.D. at 541; E.I. DuPont, 268 F.R.D. at 50-51 (“[t]he timing and circumstances of



Northern District of California
                                  16   [defendant’s] reassignment or termination of its employees render the true status of the proposed

                                  17   deponents highly suspect, and allow for a strong inference that [defendant] is moving its employees

                                  18   around like chessmen, conveniently shielding them from [plaintiff’s] access.”)). In In re Lithium

                                  19   Ion Batteries, for instance, the court applied the managing agent test to a witness even after the

                                  20   company asserted he was no longer its employee, where there was evidence that this was “a key

                                  21   witness with first-hand knowledge of the alleged conspiracy” and all but the first of the Honda

                                  22   factors weighed in favor of finding him a managing agent as of the time his deposition was

                                  23   requested. 2015 WL 5440789, at *6.
