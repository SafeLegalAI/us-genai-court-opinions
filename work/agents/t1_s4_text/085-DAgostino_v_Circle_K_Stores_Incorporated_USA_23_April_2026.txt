18                A. Circle K’s Motion to Dismiss
19                       i. Legal Standard
20            “To survive a Rule 12(b)(6) motion for failure to state a claim, a complaint must
21   meet the requirements of Rule 8(a)(2).” Rule 8(a)(2) requires a pleading to contain a “short
22   and plain statement of the claim showing that the pleader is entitled to relief.” Fed. R. Civ.
23   P. 8(a)(2). Although Rule 8 does not demand detailed factual allegations, “it demands more
24   than an unadorned, the-defendant-unlawfully-harmed-me accusation.” Ashcroft v. Iqbal,
25   556 U.S. 662, 678 (2009). “Threadbare recitals of the elements of a cause of action,
26   supported by mere conclusory statements, do not suffice.” Id. “[A] complaint must contain
27   sufficient factual matter, accepted as true, to ‘state a claim to relief that is plausible on its
28   face.’” Id. (quoting Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly, 550 U.S. 544, 570 (2007)). A claim is


                                                   -4-
     Case 2:26-cv-01225-JAT       Document 35       Filed 04/23/26     Page 5 of 22



 1   plausible “when the plaintiff pleads factual content that allows the court to draw the
 2   reasonable inference that the defendant is liable for the misconduct alleged.” Id.
 3          The Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals has instructed that courts must “construe pro se
 4   filings liberally” and give such plaintiffs the “benefit of any doubt.” Hebbe v. Pliler, 627
 5   F.3d 338, 342 (9th Cir. 2010) (internal quotations and citation omitted). A complaint filed
 6   by a pro se litigant “must be held to less stringent standards than formal pleadings drafted
 7   by lawyers.” Id. (quoting Erickson v. Pardus, 551 U.S. 89, 94 (2007) (per curiam)).
 8          Courts ordinarily may not consider evidence outside the pleadings in ruling on a
 9   Rule 12(b)(6) motion to dismiss. See United States v. Ritchie, 342 F.3d 903, 907 (9th Cir.
10   2003); Laborers Dist. Council Constr. Indus. Pension Fund v. Sea Ltd., 743 F. Supp. 3d
11   1083, 1095 (D. Ariz. 2024). Consideration of such extrinsic evidence typically converts a
12   Rule 12(b)(6) motion into a Rule 56 motion for summary judgment. Gunn v. Drage, 65
13   F.4th 1109, 1120 (9th Cir. 2023); see also Fed. R. Civ. P. 12(d) (“If, on a motion under
14   Rule 12(b)(6) . . . matters outside the pleadings are presented to and not excluded by the
15   court, the motion must be treated as one for summary judgment under Rule 56.”). However,
16   a court may consider “(1) evidence that the court has judicially noticed, and (2) evidence
17   incorporated, either literally or by reference, into the plaintiff’s complaint” without
18   converting the motion to dismiss into a summary judgment motion. Ctr. for Biological
19   Diversity v. U.S. Forest Serv., 746 F. Supp. 3d 749, 755 (D. Ariz. 2024).
20                    ii. Circle K’s Reference to Evidence Outside the Pleadings
21          In support of its Motion to Dismiss under Rule 12(b)(6), Circle K attached 72 pages

 6                   iii. Analysis re: Circle K’s Motion to Dismiss (Counts I–IV)
 7          Plaintiff’s Complaint outlines seven claims, supra page 3–4, and Counts I–IV allege
 8   violations of the TCPA. The Court will address Counts I–IV together and consider the
 9   remaining state-law claims—Counts V–VII—separately.
10                           a. Counts I–IV: Violations of the TCPA
11          Count I alleges that Circle K used an “automatic telephone dialing system”
12   (“ATDS”) to send Plaintiff text messages in violation of 47 U.S.C. § 227(b). (Doc. 1 at
13   14).
14          Counts II–IV allege claims to relief under 47 U.S.C. § 227(c). (Doc. 1 at 15–18).
15   The TCPA’s implementing regulations prohibit a person or entity from initiating any
16   telephone solicitation to (1) “[a]ny residential telephone subscriber before the hour of 8
17   a.m.” or (2) “[a] residential telephone subscriber who has registered his or her telephone
18   number on the national do-not-call registry of persons who do not wish to receive telephone
19   solicitations that is maintained by the Federal Government,” 47 C.F.R. § 64.1200(c)(1)–
20   (2); see also Abboud v. Circle K Stores Inc., 731 F. Supp. 3d 1094, 1102 (D. Ariz. 2024).
21   “Telephone solicitation” is defined as
22          the initiation of a telephone call or message for the purpose of encouraging
            the purchase or rental of, or investment in, property, goods, or services,
23          which is transmitted to any person, but such term does not include a call or
            message (A) to any person with that person’s prior express invitation or
24          permission, (B) to any person with whom the caller has an established
            business relationship, or (C) by a tax exempt nonprofit organization.
25
26   47 U.S.C. § 227(a)(4). The TCPA grants a private right of action to any consumer who
27   receives more than one call in violation of those regulations within a 12-month period. 47
28   U.S.C. § 227(c)(5).

 1          Circle K argues that Plaintiff’s TCPA claims should be dismissed because (1) the
 2   TCPA does not apply to text messages, (2) the text messages at issue were not solicitations,
 3   and (3) Plaintiff consented to receiving the text messages. The Court addresses each
 4   argument in turn.
 5                                  1. Text Messages as “Calls” Under the TCPA
 6          Circle K argues that Counts I–IV should be dismissed because he failed to allege
 7   that he received a “telephone call,” and the TCPA does not apply to text messages. (Doc.
 8   11 at 4). See 47 U.S.C. §§ 227(b)(1), (c)(5). To support this argument, Circle K cites
 9   Stockdale v. Skymount Property Group, LLC, 1:25 CV 1282, 2026 WL 591842 (N.D. Ohio
10   Mar. 3, 2026). In Stockdale, the plaintiff received text messages soliciting her to sell her
11   home “despite her number’s continued presence on the DNC Registry” and she filed suit
12   alleging violations of the TCPA. Id. at *1. The Stockdale court dismissed the claim because
13   it found that a “telephone call” under the TCPA could not include modern-day text
14   messages. Id. at *3–4.
15          In citing to Stockdale and urging this Court to accept its reasoning, Circle K failed
16   to cite binding caselaw from this Circuit arriving at the opposite conclusion. Howard v.
17   Republican Nat’l Comm., 164 F.4th 1119 (9th Cir. 2026). In Howard, the Ninth Circuit
18   Court of Appeals concluded that a “a ‘text message’ constitutes a ‘call’ within the meaning
19   of the TCPA.”6 Id. at 1123–24. Circle K acknowledges Howard for the first time in its
20   Reply, arguing that Plaintiff’s reliance on the case “is misplaced,” and that Counts I and
21   IV should be dismissed for other reasons “even assuming text messages could potentially
22   qualify as ‘calls’ under the TCPA.” (Doc. 15 at 3) (emphasis added). There is no question
23

25   [47 U.S.C.] § 227 includes a “text message.” Id. (citing Satterfield v. Simon & Schuster,
     Inc., 569 F.3d 946, 954 (9th Cir. 2009)). The U.S. Supreme Court overruled Chevron in

 1   as to whether text messages are calls under the TCPA in this Circuit. Although Circle K’s
 2   counsel seems determined to ignore it, settled law from the Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals
 3   establishes that a TCPA violation may arise from unsolicited text messages.
 4                                     2. Telephone Solicitations
 5          Circle K argues Counts I–IV should be dismissed because the messages Plaintiff
 6   received are not “telephone solicitations” under the TCPA since the messages “do not
 7   encourage the purchase or rental of, or investment in, property, goods, or services.” (Doc.
 8   11 at 3, 8); see 47 U.S.C. § 227(a)(4). Circle K contends the messages were a “continuation
 9   of enrollment messages” that are sent after a customer provides his phone number during
10   a transaction at Circle K, and that these “double opt-in messages” are meant to provide “an
11   additional second layer verification to confirm the intent of customers” who had already
12   consented to receive special offers and updates. (Doc. 11 at 3).
13          “The purpose of a text or call determines whether it qualifies as a telephone
14   solicitation, and courts use ‘a measure of common sense’ to determine its purpose.”
15   Abboud, 731 F. Supp. 3d at 1102. Text messages may constitute a telephone solicitation
16   even if the message serves a dual purpose by including “both advertising/telemarketing
17   and merely informational or transactional communications.” Id. (quoting Vallianos v.
18   Schultz, 2019 WL 4980649, *3 (W.D. Wash. 2019) (cleaned up)). Accordingly, a party
19   engaged in telephone solicitation need not directly offer property or services for sale—
20   merely encouraging the future purchase of property or services is sufficient. Id.; see Orea
21   v. Nielsen Audio, Inc., 2015 WL 1885936, at *3 (N.D. Cal. 2015) (“A call that encourages
22   future purchasing activity may be a telephone solicitation, even if the sales pitch is not
23   explicitly contained in the call itself.”).
24          The question, then, is whether it is plausible that the messages Plaintiff received
25   constituted telephone solicitations. The Complaint alleges that Plaintiff received hundreds
26   of messages that had the “objective and intent . . . to encourage the purchase of goods and
27   services at Circle K locations.” (Doc. 1 at 6 ¶ 24). Plaintiff provided screenshots of the text
28   messages, which directed him to “Reply YES to subscribe and receive special offers,


                                                   - 11 -
     Case 2:26-cv-01225-JAT       Document 35       Filed 04/23/26     Page 12 of 22



 1   updates, and more.” (Doc. 1-1). The Court concludes that “[t]hese facts make it plausible
 2   that the purpose of the text messages was to encourage Plaintiff to sign up to receive offers
 3   for future shopping at Circle K.” Abboud, 731 F. Supp. 3d at 1103–05 (three text messages
 4   advising plaintiff to reply “YES” to sign up and receive special offers from Defendant
 5   Circle K made it plausible the messages were telephone solicitations under the TCPA).
 6   Indeed, as the Abboud court noted, “it is difficult to imagine why [Circle K] would
 7   repeatedly encourage [Plaintiff] to sign up for offers other than to encourage [him] to make
 8   future purchases of goods at [Circle K’s] stores based on those offers.” Id. at 1103.
 9          Accepting the Complaint’s factual allegations as true, it is plausible that the
10   hundreds of messages Plaintiff received were telephone solicitations within the meaning
11   of the TCPA.
12                                   3. Messages Sent with Consent or Sent Pursuant to an
13                                       Established Business Relationship
14          The TCPA only prohibits unsolicited communications—a telephone solicitation

15                            a. Count V: Willful Violation of the Arizona Consumer Fraud
16                               Act
17          Plaintiff alleges that on June 10, 2025, August 12, 2025, January 9, 2026, and

19   unsubscribed from all texts on 25050 & will no longer receive text msgs. Txt YES to
20   resubscribe. Q’s? Txt HELP. Msg&Data rates may apply.” (Doc. 1 at 7 ¶ 29; Doc. 1 at 20
21   ¶ 103). After sending the June 10, 2025 confirmation that Plaintiff would be unsubscribed
22   from future text messages, Circle K transmitted 308 additional messages to Plaintiff. (Doc.
23   1 at 9 ¶ 35). Plaintiff alleges that in doing so, Circle K violated the Arizona Consumer
24   Fraud Act. (Doc. 1 at 19–20).
25          “The purpose of the [Arizona Consumer Fraud] Act is to provide injured consumers
26   with a remedy to counteract the disproportionate bargaining power often present in
27   10
            As discussed above, supra page 7 n.2, the Court granted Plaintiff’s sur-reply motion
     (Doc. 16) and considered the lodged sur-reply (Doc. 17) to the extent that Plaintiff rebutted

                                                - 15 -

 1   consumer transactions.” Waste Mfg. & Leasing Corp. v. Hambicki, 900 P.2d 1220, 1224
 2   (Ariz. Ct. App. 1995). The Arizona Consumer Fraud Act (the “CFA”) provides as follows:
 3           The act, use or employment by any person of any deception, deceptive or
             unfair act or practice, fraud, false pretense, false promise, misrepresentation,
 4           or concealment, suppression or omission of any material fact with intent that
             others rely on such concealment, suppression or omission, in connection with
 5           the sale or advertisement of any merchandise whether or not any person has

 6           unlawful practice.
 7   A.R.S. § 44-1522(A).
 8           The elements of private cause of action under the CFA are “[1] are a false promise
 9   or misrepresentation [2] made in connection with the sale or advertisement of merchandise
10   and [3] the [plaintiff’s] consequent and proximate injury.” Castle v. Barrett-Jackson
11   Auction Co., LLC, 276 P.3d 540, 542 (Ariz. Ct. App. 2012). The statute requires that a
12   person intend for others to rely on the false promise or misrepresentation “in connection
13   with the sale or advertisement of any merchandise.” A.R.S. § 44-1522(A). Accordingly,
14   there must be a sale between the parties or an advertisement in relation to a sale between
15   the parties to support a cause of action under the CFA. See Sullivan v. Pulte Home Corp.,
16   290 P.3d 446, 453–54 ¶¶ 36–37 (Ariz. Ct. App. 2012), vacated in part, 306 P.3d 1 (Ariz.
17   2013) (vacating ¶¶ 24–31).
18           Plaintiff alleges that that the four messages confirming he had been unsubscribed

22   K] to continue their unauthorized access to Plaintiffs cellular device for marketing profit.”

24   conduct, including “the economic value of 50+ hours of time, resource depletion, and
25   systemic sleep deprivation” caused by 146 early-morning text messages. (Doc. 1 at 20 ¶

28   private right of action under the CFA, but argues that Count V should be dismissed because

 1   Plaintiff fails to identify any “sale or advertisement of any merchandise” regarding the opt-

 4   from all texts” and “will no longer receive text msgs.” (Doc. 1 at 7 ¶ 29). At most, the

23                              b. Count VI: Common Law Intrusion Upon Seclusion
24            Plaintiff alleges that Circle K intentionally and repeatedly intruded upon his
25   “solitude, seclusion, and private affairs by transmitting 350 unauthorized marketing
26   solicitations to his private cellular telephone.” (Doc. 1 at 22 ¶ 110). Plaintiff contends that
27   the nature and frequency of the messages “would be highly offensive to any reasonable
28   person.” (Doc. 1 at 22 ¶ 111). In support of this claim, Plaintiff cites to Navient Solutions,

     Case 2:26-cv-01225-JAT       Document 35       Filed 04/23/26     Page 18 of 22



 1   LLC v. Law Offices of Jeffrey Lohman, 2020 WL 1864871, at *12 (D. Ariz. 2020). This
 2   case does not exist. As Circle K notes in its Motion to Dismiss, a search of “2020 WL
 3   1864871” returns an Ohio Public Utilities Commission Finding and Order titled “In the
 4   Matter of the Proper Procedures and Process for the Commission’s Operations and

 7   Virginia. See Navient Sols., LLC v. Law Offices of Jeffrey Lohman, 1:19-CV-461
 8   (LMB/TCB), 2020 WL 1867939 (E.D. Va. Apr. 14, 2020). However, that case is irrelevant
 9   to Plaintiff’s claim because it is devoid of any discussion regarding the tort of inclusion
10   upon seclusion.
11          Circle K argues Count VI should be dismissed because Plaintiff fails to provide any
12   basis in Arizona law for his “intrusion of seclusion” claim. (Doc. 11 at 13). In response,
13   Plaintiff argues that the tort of intrusion upon seclusion is supported by “decades of state
14   jurisprudence.” (Doc. 13 at 12).
15          Arizona state courts recognize four invasion-of-privacy claims, including the tort of
16   intrusion upon seclusion, Hart v. Seven Resorts Inc., 947 P.2d 846, 853 (Ariz. Ct. App.
17   1997), which “occurs when one intentionally intrudes, physically or otherwise, upon the
18   solitude or seclusion of another or his private affairs or concerns and the intrusion is highly
19   offensive to a reasonable person,” Shepherd v. Costco Wholesale Corp., 441 P.3d 989, 995
20   (App. 2019), vacated in part, 482 P.3d 390 (Ariz. 2021) (vacating ¶¶ 25–35) (internal
21   quotations and citation omitted). A defendant is “subject to liability . . . only when he has
22   intruded into a private place, or has otherwise invaded a private seclusion that the plaintiff
23   has thrown about his person or affairs.” Hart, 947 P.2d at 853 (quoting Restatement
24   (Second) of Torts § 652B). Intrusion upon seclusion usually involves
25          (1) physical intrusion into a place in which the plaintiff has secluded himself;
26          (2) the use of the defendant’s senses, with or without mechanical aids, to
            oversee or overhear the plaintiff’s private affairs, as by looking into his
27          upstairs windows with binoculars or tapping his telephone wires; or
28          (3) some other form of investigation or examination into his private concerns,
            as by opening his private and personal mail, searching his safe or his wallet,

                                                 - 18 -
     Case 2:26-cv-01225-JAT       Document 35       Filed 04/23/26       Page 19 of 22



 1          examining his private bank account, or compelling him by a forged court
            order to permit an inspection of his personal documents.
 2
 3   Shepherd, 441 P.3d at 995 (internal quotations and citation omitted). Conclusory
 4   allegations that a defendant’s conduct intruded upon the plaintiff’s solitude and seclusion,
 5   without more, are insufficient to support a claim for relief. Id.
 6           Plaintiff claims that the “transmission of 350 messages over several months,
 7   including 146 messages during hours of rest, represents . . . a substantial burden to
 8   Plaintiff[‘]s existence.” (Doc. 1 at 23, ¶ 111). He further alleges that Circle K, by initiating
 9   146 solicitations—some of which occurred between 4:11 a.m. and 5:30 a.m.—intentionally
10   violated his right to peace and solitude, causing “severe physiological stress and systemic
11   sleep deprivation.” (Doc. 1 at 23, ¶ 112).
12          Plaintiff does not cite, and this Court’s independent research did not reveal, any
13   Arizona caselaw regarding the tort of intrusion upon seclusion as it relates to unwanted text
14   messages. However, the Restatement (Second) of Torts provides some support for
15   Plaintiff’s position that repeated, incessant text messages can support an invasion of
16   privacy claim. The Restatement provides:
17          [T]here is no liability for [the tort of intrusion upon seclusion for] knocking
            at the plaintiff’s door, or calling him to the telephone on one occasion or even
18          two or three, to demand payment of a debt. It is only when the telephone calls
            are repeated with such persistence and frequency as to amount to a course
19          of hounding the plaintiff, that becomes a substantial burden to his existence,
            that his privacy is invaded.
20
21   Restatement (Second) of Torts § 652B, cmt. d (emphasis added).
22          And the Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals has found that auto-dialed text messages
23   typically result in “an immediate intrusion in the form of a display of a preview of the text
24   on the phone’s screen (even if locked), a sound, a vibration, or some combination of those,”
25   thereby producing “the particular type of intrusion on privacy at which the [TCPA] is
26   aimed, which is an intrusion upon ‘seclusion.’” Howard, 164 F.4th at 1124. The Eleventh
27   Circuit Court of Appeals has also indicated that unwanted text messages, like unwanted
28   phone calls, are among the privacy intrusions giving rise to liability for intrusion upon

 1   seclusion. See Drazen v. Pinto, 74 F.4th 1336, 1345 (11th Cir. 2023) (“And as with ‘the
 2   unwanted ringing of a phone from a phone call,’ the ‘undesired buzzing of a cell phone
 3   from a text message . . . is an intrusion into peace and quiet in a realm that is private and
 4   personal.’”) (citation omitted). The Drazen court acknowledged, however, that it is difficult
 5   to draw the line in determining how many unwanted text messages are required before the
 6   intrusion becomes “highly offensive to a reasonable person.” See id.

 3          Plaintiff’s Complaint is devoid of any support for his novel theory that that he and

 5   contractually bound or that the doctrine of promissory estoppel applies to these facts.
 6          “An implied-in-fact contract is a true contract, differing from an express contract
 7   only insofar as it is proved by circumstantial evidence rather than by express written or oral
 8   terms.” Pyeatte v. Pyeatte, 661 P.2d 196, 203 (Ariz. Ct. App. 1982). To support an
 9   “implied-in-fact” contract claim, a party must still demonstrate the elements of a binding
10   contract, including “mutual assent or certainty as to the critical terms of the agreement.”
11   Id. When “a reasonable person” could conclude that both parties intended to contract for
12   certain terms, an implied-in-fact contract is formed. See Demasse v. ITT Corp., 984 P.2d
13   1138, 1143 (Ariz. 1999).

15   a company’s messaging [system] would ‘create contractual obligations.’” (Doc. 11 at 14).

18   contract or constitutes the acceptance of a unilateral offer, or (2) that a company’s
19   confirmation of an opt-out request constitutes a “promise” to support a promissory estoppel
20   claim. (Doc. 11 at 15). Plaintiff failed to identify any such authority and the Court’s
21   independent research similarly failed to reveal any law supporting Count VII. “[A]lthough
22   Plaintiff appears in this Court pro se, the Court is not required to scour his Complaint and
23   piece together his claims for him.” Moore v. Maricopa Cnty. Juvenile Court, CV-18-
24   01553-PHX-JJT, 2018 WL 9848169, at *1 (D. Ariz. Sept. 18, 2018), aff’d, 786 Fed. Appx.
25   89 (9th Cir. 2019). Indeed, plaintiffs are responsible for identifying the legal basis for the
26   claims identified in their complaints. See id. at *2. Given Plaintiff’s failure to do so, the
27   Court will dismiss Count VII.
