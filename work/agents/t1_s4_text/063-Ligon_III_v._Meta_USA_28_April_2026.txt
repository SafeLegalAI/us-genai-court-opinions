                                  23                                          LEGAL STANDARD

                                  24          Under Rule 12(b)(6) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure, a complaint may be

                                  25   dismissed for failure to state a claim for which relief may be granted. Rule 12(b)(6) requires

                                  26   dismissal when a complaint lacks either a “cognizable legal theory” or “sufficient facts alleged” in

                                  27   support of such a theory. Godecke v. Kinetic Concepts, Inc., 937 F.3d 1201, 1208 (9th Cir. 2019)

                                  28   (citation omitted). A complaint contains sufficient factual allegations if it pleads enough facts to
                                                                                         6
                                   1   “state a claim to relief that is plausible on its face.” Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556 U.S. 662, 678 (2009)

                                   2   (quoting Bell Atl. Corp. v. Twombly, 550 U.S. 544, 570 (2007)). In turn, a claim is plausible

                                   3   “when the plaintiff pleads factual content that allows the court to draw the reasonable inference

                                   4   that the defendant is liable for the misconduct alleged.” Id. at 678. Plausibility is a context-specific

                                   5   task that requires the reviewing court to determine if the well-pleaded facts permit the court to

                                   6   infer more than the mere possibility of misconduct. Id.

                                   7          When evaluating a motion to dismiss, courts “accept factual allegations in the complaint as

                                   8   true and construe the pleadings in the light most favorable to the nonmoving party.” Manzarek v.

                                   9   St. Paul Fire & Marine Ins. Co., 519 F.3d 1025, 1031 (9th Cir. 2008). However, “allegations in a

                                  10   complaint . . . may not simply recite the elements of a cause of action [and] must contain sufficient

                                  11   allegations of underlying facts to give fair notice and to enable the opposing party to defend itself

                                  12   effectively.” Levitt v. Yelp! Inc., 765 F.3d 1123, 1135 (9th Cir. 2014) (quoting Eclectic Props. E.,



 United States District Court
                                  13   LLC v. Marcus & Millichap Co., 751 F.3d 990, 996 (9th Cir. 2014)).

                                  14          Pro se pleadings are to be liberally construed, but they still must meet basic plausibility

                                  15   standards and provide the minimum factual basis necessary to give defendants notice of the claims



Northern District of California
                                  16   against them. See Turner v. Cnty. of Los Angeles, 18 F. App’x 592, 596 (9th Cir. 2001) (“Even

                                  17   given the more generous pleading standards for pro se plaintiffs, [plaintiff] has failed to provide a

                                  18   minimum factual basis needed to provide notice to these defendants.”); Ortez v. Washington Cnty.,

                                  19   88 F.3d 804, 807 (9th Cir. 1996) (pro se pleadings must be construed liberally on Rule 12(b)(6)

                                  20   motions, such that the court should determine whether the plaintiff would not be entitled to relief

                                  21   “under any state of facts he could prove”). When dismissing a claim, a court “should grant leave to

                                  22   amend even if no request to amend the pleading was made, unless it determines that the pleading

                                  23   could not possibly be cured by other facts.” Lopez v. Smith, 203 F.3d 1122, 1127 (9th Cir. 2000).

                                  13       B. Section 230 Limits on Liability

                                  14           Defendant first moves to dismiss the FAC on the grounds that every claim in the complaint

                                  15   is barred by Section 230(c)(1) of the Communications Decency Act, 47 U.S.C. § 230(c)(1).



Northern District of California
                                  16   Section 230, titled “Protection for private blocking and screening of offensive material,” contains

                                  17   the following provisions: “No provider or user of an interactive computer service shall be treated

                                  18   as the publisher or speaker of any information provided by another information content provider . .

                                  19   . No cause of action may be brought and no liability may be imposed under any State or local law

                                  20   that is inconsistent with this section.” 47 U.S.C. § 230(c)(1), (e)(3). Thus, Section 230(c)(1)

                                  21   immunity applies to “(1) a provider or user of an interactive computer service, (2) whom a

                                  22   plaintiff seeks to treat, under a [federal or] state law cause of action, as a publisher or speaker, (3)

                                  23   of information provided by another information content provider.” Calise v. Meta Platforms, Inc.,

                                  24   103 F.4th 732, 738 (9th Cir. 2024) (quoting Barnes v. Yahoo!, Inc., 570 F.3d 1096, 1100–01 (9th

                                  22   Defendant Meta is a state actor for constitutional purposes. The First Amendment only prohibits

                                  23   the governmental abridgment of speech and does not apply to private actors except in those

                                  24   “exceptional cases” where a private party may be considered a state actor for the purposes of an

                                  25   alleged constitutional deprivation. Children’s Health Def. v. Meta Platforms, Inc., 112 F.4th 742,

                                  26   753 (9th Cir. 2024), cert. denied, 145 S. Ct. 2846 (2025); O’Handley v. Weber, 62 F.4th 1145,

                                   1   1156 (9th Cir. 2023). The Fifth Amendment’s due process clause also applies exclusively to the

                                   2   federal government, Bingue v. Prunchak, 512 F.3d 1169, 1174 (9th Cir. 2008), and it is only

                                   3   extended to private actors in the rare circumstance that there is “a sufficiently close nexus between

                                   4   the (government) and the challenged action of the . . . (private) entity so that the action of the latter

                                   5   may be fairly treated as that of the (government) itself.” Rank v. Nimmo, 677 F.2d 692, 701 (9th

                                   6   Cir. 1982) (quoting Warren v. Gov’t Nat’l Mortg. Ass’n, 611 F.2d 1229, 1232 (8th Cir. 1980))

                                   7   (alterations in original). A private party may be treated as a government actor when it meets both

                                   8   the “state policy” requirement, under which the alleged constitutional violation must result from a

                                   9   right created by the state or a rule of conduct imposed by the state, and the “state actor”

                                  10   requirement, which may be satisfied by showing that “(1) the private actor performs a traditionally

                                  11   public function; (2) the private actor is a ‘willful participant in joint activity’ with the government;

                                  18   5. Claim Six: Breach of the Implied Covenant of Good Faith and Fair Dealing

                                  19          Plaintiff’s sixth claim states that Defendant violated the implied covenant of good faith and

                                  20   fair dealing “by suspending Plaintiff’s ads and accounts in bad faith, without providing specific

                                  21   violations despite appeals, and targeting conservative content while allowing similar ads from

                                  22   other viewpoints.” (FAC ¶ 45.) Plaintiff has failed to state a claim for breach of the implied

                                  23   covenant of good faith and fair dealing based on both the suspension and deletion of his content

                                  24   and accounts and based on Defendant’s process for notifying him of their publishing decisions and

                                  25   responding to his appeals.

                                  26          The implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing is tied to an existing specific

                                  27   contractual obligation—it is well established that “the scope of conduct prohibited by the covenant

                                  28   of good faith is circumscribed by the purposes and express terms of the contract.” Carma Devs.
                                                                                         30
                                   1   (Cal.), Inc. v. Marathon Dev. California, Inc., 826 P.2d 710, 727 (Cal. 1992); accord Lewis v.

                                   2   Google LLC, 461 F. Supp. 3d 938, 961 (N.D. Cal. 2020). In California, claims for breach of this

                                   3   implied covenant must allege the specific contract term that was frustrated by Defendant’s

                                   4   conduct. This claim suffers from the same deficiencies as the breach of contract claim—he has not

                                   5   identified the specific contract provisions that were thwarted, and he has not alleged facts to

                                   6   support the finding that those provisions were enforceable contract terms rather than general

                                   7   policies. Moreover, the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing cannot require a party to

                                   8   go beyond the terms of a contract and cannot contradict the express terms. See Song fi Inc. v.

                                   9   Google, Inc., 108 F. Supp. 3d 876, 885 (N.D. Cal. 2015) (“[I]f defendants were given the right to

                                  10   do what they did by the express provisions of the contract there can be no breach.” (quoting

                                  11   Carma Devs., 826 P.2d at 728)). Here, Defendant’s Advertising Standards specifically give

                                  12   Defendant the right to reject ads “for any reason, in [its] sole discretion.” (Duffy Decl. Ex. C, Dkt.



 United States District Court
                                  13   50-4, at 14.) Thus, Defendant is explicitly allowed under the terms of the contract to reject

                                  14   Plaintiff’s ads for any reason whenever it wants.

                                  15           Finally, Plaintiff’s claims that Defendant violated the implied covenant through its non-



Northern District of California
                                  16   functional appeals process is also not borne out by the facts as alleged. Plaintiff states that he

                                  17   replied to the emails about the suspension of his ads requesting further information and that the

                                  18   links to the appeals portal were broken, and that despite his entreaties he was never given further

                                  19   explanation as to the rejections of his content. While this is unfortunate, the Advertising Standards

                                  20   explicitly include the means for requesting review of ad rejections—through requesting a review

                                  21   “in Account Quality”—and do not guarantee that requests for review will be granted or that

                                  22   specific violations information will be provided to advertisers. (Id. at 5.) “[I]t is well settled the

                                  23   implied covenant does not extend so far as to impose enforceable duties that are beyond the scope

                                  24   of the contract, nor does the covenant prohibit actions that are expressly authorized by the

                                  25   contract’s terms.” Jenkins v. JPMorgan Chase Bank, N.A., 156 Cal. Rptr. 3d 912, 935 (2013), as

                                  26   modified (June 12, 2013), disapproved of on other grounds by Yvanova v. New Century Mortg.

                                  27   Corp., 365 P.3d 845 (Cal. 2016). The express contract here does not require that Plaintiff be given

                                  28   detailed information or an opportunity to appeal if his ads are rejected, and it expressly allows
                                                                                          31
                                   1   Defendant to reject ads for any reason in its sole discretion. Without more, the factual assertions

                                   2   do not state a claim for breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing. As with the

                                   3   breach of contract claim, although the court is skeptical that Plaintiff can successfully amend this

                                   4   claim, it cannot be categorically ruled out. As such, Claim Six is DISMISSED with leave to

                                   5   amend.

                                   6   6. Claim Seven: Discrimination in Contracts under 42 U.S.C. § 1981

                                   7            Plaintiff’s seventh claim alleges that Defendant violated 42 U.S.C. § 1981 because

                                   8   “Defendant’s actions—approving his ads, accepting payment, then suspending them without cause

                                   9   while permitting liberal ads—denied him equal contractual rights, motivated by viewpoint bias.”

                                  10   (FAC ¶ 48.) Section 1981 prohibits only racial discrimination in the making and enforcement of

                                  11   private contracts. E.g., Shah v. Mt. Zion Hosp. & Med. Ctr., 642 F.2d 268, 272 (9th Cir. 1981).

                                  12   Plaintiff has not alleged any facts to suggest that Defendant racially discriminated against him or



 United States District Court
                                  13   that his race was the reason he was allegedly denied equal contractual rights, and thus he has failed

                                  14   to state a claim under section 1981. Plaintiff’s complaint contains no hint that he could amend this

                                  15   claim to allege racial discrimination, and this claim is barred by section 230(c)(1); accordingly,



Northern District of California
                                  16   Claim Seven is DISMISSED without leave to amend.
