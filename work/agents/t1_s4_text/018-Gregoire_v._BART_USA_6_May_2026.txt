                                  14                                       III.   LEGAL STANDARD

                                  15          Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 11(b) provides in part that, in presenting to a court a




Northern District of California
                                  16   written and signed pleading, motion, or other paper, an attorney “certifies that to the best of the

                                  17   person's knowledge, information, and belief, formed after an inquiry reasonable under the

                                  18   circumstances . . . the claims, defenses, and other legal contentions are warranted by existing law

                                  19   or by a nonfrivolous argument for extending, modifying, or reversing existing law or for

                                  20   establishing new law.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 11(b)(2). Under Rule 11(b), an attorney who signs a legal

                                  21   document certifies that they have “read the document, [ ] conducted a reasonable inquiry into the

                                  22   facts and the law and is satisfied that the document is well grounded in both, and is acting without

                                  23   any improper motive.” Bus. Guides, Inc. v. Chromatic Commc’ns Enters., Inc., 498 U.S. 533, 542

                                  24   (1991); see also Park v. Kim, 91 F.4th 610, 615 (2d Cir. 2024) (“At the very least, the duties

                                  25   imposed by Rule 11 require that attorneys read, and thereby confirm the existence and validity of,

                                  26   the legal authorities on which they rely.” (citation omitted)). Rule 11(b) thus imposes on counsel

                                  27   an affirmative duty to investigate the caselaw they cite before submitting a court filing. Rachel v.

                                  28   Banana Republic, Inc., 831 F.2d 1503, 1508 (9th Cir. 1987).

                                                                                         2
                                        Case 3:25-cv-02150-TSH            Document 45         Filed 05/06/26     Page 3 of 7




                                   1          Courts apply an objective test in assessing whether Rule 11(b) has been violated. Yagman

                                   2   v. Republic Ins., 987 F.2d 622, 628 (9th Cir. 1993) (citing Zaldivar v. City of Los Angeles, 780

                                   3   F.2d 823, 829-32 (9th Cir. 1986), abrogated on other grounds, 496 U.S. 384 (1990)). “A

                                   4   violation of the rule does not require subjective bad faith.” Id. (citing Zaldivar, 780 F.2d at 829-

                                   5   32). If a court determines that Rule 11(b) has been violated, it “may impose an appropriate

                                   6   sanction on [the] attorney . . . responsible for the violation.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 11(c)(1).

                                   7          In addition to Rule 11’s obligations, Rule 3.1 of the Rules of Professional Conduct of the

                                   8   State Bar of California provides that a lawyer shall not “present a claim or defense in litigation

                                   9   that is not warranted under existing law, unless it can be supported by a good faith argument for an

                                  10   extension, modification, or reversal of the existing law.” Cal. R. Prof. Conduct 3.1(a)(2).

                                  11   Moreover, Rule 3.3 requires candor to the tribunal. That rule specifically provides that a lawyer

                                  12   shall not “knowingly make a false statement of fact or law to a tribunal or fail to correct a false



 United States District Court
                                  13   statement of material fact or law previously made to the tribunal by the lawyer.”

                                  14                                            IV.    DISCUSSION
                                  15   A.     Citations to Fictitious Cases



Northern District of California
                                  16          “‘To start with the obvious, an attorney who submits fake cases clearly has not read those

                                  17   nonexistent cases.’” Oneto v. Watson, 808 F. Supp. 3d 974, 978 (N.D. Cal. 2025) (emphasis in

                                  18   original) (quoting Benjamin v. Costco Wholesale Corp., 779 F. Supp. 3d 341, 343 (E.D.N.Y.

                                  19   2025)). Without reading the legal authorities upon which she relied, Ms. Barsotti could not have

                                  20   ensured “that the arguments made based on those authorities are ‘warranted by existing law,’ or

                                  21   otherwise ‘legally tenable.’” Park, 91 F.4th at 615 (citations omitted); see also Mata v. Avianca,

                                  22   Inc., 678 F. Supp. 3d 443, 461 (S.D.N.Y. 2023) (“A fake opinion is not ‘existing law’ and citation

                                  23   to a fake opinion does not provide a non-frivolous ground for extending, modifying, or reversing

                                  24   existing law, or for establishing new law.”). Ms. Barsotti’s “submission of citations to nonexistent

                                  25   cases, on its own, constitutes a violation of FRCP 11(b).” Oneto, 808 F. Supp. 3d at 978

                                  26   (collecting cases).

                                  27          Ms. Barsotti’s erroneous citations make clear she submitted Plaintiff’s brief to the Court

                                  28   without conducting a reasonable inquiry into the validity of the legal authorities cited because she
                                                                                          3
                                        Case 3:25-cv-02150-TSH            Document 45          Filed 05/06/26     Page 4 of 7




                                   1   otherwise would have recognized that the cases cited do not exist. Ms. Barsotti nonetheless signed

                                   2   and filed Plaintiff’s brief, falsely certifying she had conducted a “reasonable inquiry into the facts

                                   3   and the law,” Bus. Guides, Inc., 498 U.S. at 551, and that the filing’s “legal contentions” were

                                   4   “warranted by existing law or by a nonfrivolous argument for extending, modifying, or reversing

                                   5   existing law or for establishing new law,” Fed. R. Civ. P. 11(b)(2). By including such unchecked

                                   6   authorities in her submission, Ms. Barsotti violated Rule 11. Further, Ms. Barsotti’s submission of

                                   7   fictitious cases to the Court “not only violated Rule 11, it degraded or impugned the integrity of

                                   8   the Court and it also violated California Rules of Professional Conduct 3.1(a)(2), 3.3(a)(1), and

                                   9   3.3(a)(2).” Oneto, 808 F. Supp. 3d at 978-79 (cleaned up) (quoting United States v. Hayes, 763 F.

                                  10   Supp. 3d 1054, 1064 (E.D. Cal. 2025) (noting that the submission of fictitious legal authority

                                  11   violates rules of professional conduct)).

                                  12           To be clear, the Court does not prohibit or oppose the use of artificial intelligence in legal



 United States District Court
                                  13   advocacy, so long as counsel independently verifies the accuracy of AI-generated content as

                                  14   required under Rule 11. Ms. Barsotti did not so certify here, and she has provided no reason for

                                  15   her failure to do so. This failure falls short of her obligations and “it further suggests a failure to



Northern District of California
                                  16   keep abreast of changes in relevant technology as required by Rule 1.1 of the California Rules of

                                  17   Professional Conduct on attorney competence.” Oneto, 808 F. Supp. 3d at 979 (citing Cal. R.

                                  18   Prof. Conduct 1.1, comment 1).

                                  19   B.      Sanctions Against Ms. Barsotti
                                  20           Where a court finds an attorney violated Rule 11(b), it “may impose an appropriate

                                  21   sanction” after the court has provided that attorney “notice and a reasonable opportunity to

                                  22   respond.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 11(c)(1). “[A]n opportunity to be heard does not require an oral or

                                  23   evidentiary hearing on the issue.” Pac. Harbor Cap., Inc. v. Carnival Air Lines, Inc., 210 F.3d

                                  24   1112, 1118 (9th Cir. 2000). Sanctions for violation of Rule 11(b) may be imposed via motion or

                                  25   on a court’s own initiative. Fed. R. Civ. P. 11(c)(2)-(3). “[U]nlike the situation in which an

                                  26   opposing party moves for Rule 11 sanctions – there is no ‘safe harbor’ in the Rule allowing

                                  27   lawyers to correct or withdraw their challenged filings” when a court considers issuing sanctions

                                  28   sua sponte. United Nat. Ins. Co. v. R&D Latex Corp., 242 F.3d 1102, 1115-16 (9th Cir. 2001)
                                                                                           4
                                        Case 3:25-cv-02150-TSH            Document 45          Filed 05/06/26      Page 5 of 7




                                   1   (citation omitted). In light of this distinction, sanctions issued sua sponte under Rule 11 “will

                                   2   ordinarily be imposed only in situations that are akin to a contempt of court.” Id. at 1116

                                   3   (emphasis in original, citation omitted). “The district court has wide discretion in determining the

                                   4   appropriate sanction for a Rule 11 violation.” Hudson v. Moore Bus. Forms, Inc., 836 F.2d 1156,

                                   5   1163 (9th Cir. 1987). “‘Rule 11 sanctions are meant to serve several purposes, including (1)

                                   6   deterring future litigation abuse, (2) punishing present litigation abuse, (3) compensating victims

                                   7   of litigation abuse, and (4) streamlining court dockets and facilitating case management.’” Oneto,

                                   8   808 F. Supp. 3d at 979 (quoting Wadsworth v. Walmart Inc., 348 F.R.D. 489 496-97 (D. Wyo.

                                   9   2025)).

                                  12   for her use of nonexistent and erroneous citations in Plaintiff’s opposition brief. See ECF No. 42.
