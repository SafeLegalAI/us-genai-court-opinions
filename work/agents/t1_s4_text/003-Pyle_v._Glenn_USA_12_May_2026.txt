24   II.    LEGAL STANDARDS

25          A.     Rule 8, Pro Se Pleadings, Construction and Amendment

26          Pro se pleadings are to be liberally construed and afforded the benefit of any

27   doubt. Chambers v. Herrera, 78 F.4th 1100, 1104 (9th Cir. 2023). However, the court

28   need not accept as true conclusory allegations, unreasonable inferences, or
                                                   4
1    unwarranted deductions of fact. Western Mining Council v. Watt, 643 F.2d 618, 624 (9th

2    Cir. 1981). To give fair notice of the claims and the grounds on which they rest, a plaintiff

3    must allege with at least some degree of particularity overt acts by specific defendants

4    which support the claims. See Kimes v. Stone, 84 F.3d 1121, 1129 (9th Cir. 1996). A

5    formulaic recitation of the elements of a cause of action does not suffice to state a claim.

6    Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly, 550 U.S. 544, 555-57 (2007); Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556 U.S.

7    662, 678 (2009). To state a claim on which relief may be granted, the plaintiff must

8    allege enough facts “to state a claim to relief that is plausible on its face.” Twombly, 550

9    U.S. at 570. “A claim has facial plausibility when the plaintiff pleads factual content that

10   allows the court to draw the reasonable inference that the defendant is liable for the

11   misconduct alleged.” Iqbal, 556 U.S. at 678.

12          Upon dismissal of any claims, the court must tell a pro se plaintiff of a pleading’s

13   deficiencies and provide an opportunity to cure such defects. Garity v. APWU Nat'l Lab.

14   Org., 828 F.3d 848, 854 (9th Cir. 2016). However, if amendment would be futile, leave to

15   amend does not need to be provided. Lathus v. City of Huntington Beach, 56 F.4th 1238,

16   1243 (9th Cir. 2023).

17          To determine the propriety of a dismissal motion, the court may not consider facts

18   raised outside the complaint (such as in an opposition brief), but it may consider such

19   facts when deciding whether to grant leave to amend. Broam v. Bogan, 320 F.3d 1023,

20   1026 n.2 (9th Cir. 2003).

21          B.     Failure to State a Claim under Rule 12(b)(6)

22          A claim may be dismissed because of the plaintiff’s “failure to state a claim upon

23   which relief can be granted.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 12(b)(6). A complaint fails to state a claim if

24   it either lacks a cognizable legal theory or sufficient facts to support a cognizable legal

25   theory. Mollett v. Netflix, Inc., 795 F.3d 1062, 1065 (9th Cir. 2015). When considering

26   whether a claim has been stated, the court must accept the well-pleaded factual

27   allegations as true and construe the complaint in the light most favorable to the non-

28   moving party. Id. However, the court is not required to accept as true conclusory factual
                                                   5
1    allegations contradicted by documents referenced in the complaint, or legal conclusions

2    merely because they are cast in the form of factual allegations. Paulsen v. CNF Inc., 559

3    F.3d 1061, 1071 (9th Cir. 2009).

23          B.     Motion to Dismiss

24          Defendants move to dismiss the SAC for (1) failure to present a short and plain

25   statement of Plaintiff’s claims pursuant to Rule 8(a)(2), and (2) failure to allege sufficient

26   facts to support a claim pursuant to Rule 12(b)(6). Def. Mot. at 4-5, 5-8. Defendants

27   argue that the SAC does not comply with Rule 8 because it fails to identify who are the

28   defendant parties and does not clearly indicate what, if any other, causes of action
                                                   6
1    Plaintiff asserts apart from a Section 1983 claim based solely on a Monell theory. Id. at

2    1, 5. Defendants argue that, even if the SAC satisfies Rule 8, it should be dismissed

3    pursuant to Rule 12(b)(6) because: (1) any claims against Defendant Gibbs in his official

4    capacity must be dismissed as duplicative of Plaintiff’s Monell claim; (2) any claims

5    against Defendant Gibbs in his individual capacity are based on conclusory, speculative

6    allegations; and (3) the claim based on Monell liability fails to allege facts to identify or

7    support an unconstitutional custom, policy, or practice by Defendants. Id. at 5-8.

8                  1.      Rule 8 Pleading Requirements

9           Plaintiff's SAC does not contain a short and plain statement of a claim as required

10   by Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 8 because the SAC fails to allege, with at least some

11   degree of particularity, overt acts by specific defendants which support Plaintiff’s claims.

12   See Kimes, 84 F.3d at 1129.

13          As an initial problem, Plaintiff has not adequately identified the defendants to the

14   SAC. The caption page provides “Cheif [sic] Justin Gibbs [line break] Glenn County

15   Sheriff Officer Doe” as the Defendants, but nowhere in the SAC does Plaintiff clarify

16   whether he is suing one defendant (Defendant Gibbs), two (Defendants Gibbs and

17   Glenn County Sheriff’s Department), or three or more defendants, including Doe

18   defendants. See SAC at 1. Under the “Cause of Action” heading, the SAC appears to

19   allege a Section 1983 claim based on Monell liability, but there it names “[t]he County of

20   Glenn” rather than the Glenn County Sheriff’s Department, which was named in the

21   FAC. Id. at 4 ¶ 7. Under the “Prayer for Relief” heading, the SAC refers to “Defendants,”

22   “the Glenn County Sheriff’s Office,” and “County of Glenn.” Id. at 7:15-28, 8. Thus,

23   because the defendants to the SAC are not clearly identified, the SAC fails to satisfy the

24   requirements of Rule 8.

25          Further, nowhere in the SAC does Plaintiff clearly identify (apart from the Monell

26   claim) what other claim(s), if any, Plaintiff brings, what defendants against whom Plaintiff

27   seeks to bring such claim(s), and on what theory of liability. “[C]ourts have routinely

28   granted motions to dismiss” where defendants “lack notice as to which theory of liability
                                                    7
1    Plaintiffs are advancing against which of them and cannot make informed responses as

2    to Plaintiffs' allegations.” Taylor v. City of Torrance, 2025 WL 4059416, at *3 (C.D. Cal.

3    Oct. 8, 2025). Plaintiff’s opposition fails to address these omissions. See Pl. Opp’n at 3-

4    4. As the Court earlier cautioned Plaintiff, the SAC must not require the Court and

5    defendants to guess at what is being alleged against whom. See 10/14/2025 Order and

6    Findings and Recommendations at 10; McHenry v. Renne, 84 F.3d 1172, 1177 (9th Cir.

7    1996) (affirming dismissal of a complaint where the district court was “literally guessing

8    as to what facts support the legal claims being asserted against certain defendants”).

9           Accordingly, because the SAC fails to comply with Rule 8 in providing notice to

10   Defendants of what claims are being brought against them, the Court recommends the

11   SAC be dismissed.

12                 2.     Monell Claim

13          Defendants argue that any claim against the Glenn County Sheriff’s Department

14   based on Monell liability fails because the SAC does not allege facts to identify or

15   support an unconstitutional custom, policy, or practice by Defendants. Def. Mot. at 5-8.

16          42 U.S.C. § 1983 “provides a cause of action for the deprivation of any rights,

17   privileges, or immunities secured by the Constitution and laws of the United States.”

18   Wilder v. Virginia Hosp. Ass'n, 496 U.S. 498, 508 (1990) (internal quotation marks

19   omitted). “Section 1983 is not itself a source of substantive rights but merely provides a

20   method for vindicating federal rights elsewhere conferred.” Albright v. Oliver, 510 U.S.

21   266, 271 (1994) (citation and internal quotation marks omitted). To state a cognizable

22   Section 1983 claim, a plaintiff must allege the violation of a right protected by the

23   Constitution and laws of the United States, and that the alleged deprivation was

24   committed by a person who acted under color of state law. 42 U.S.C. § 1983; see also

25   Florer v. Congregation Pidyon Shevuyim, N.A., 639 F.3d 916, 921 (9th Cir. 2011). An

26   individual defendant is not liable on a civil rights claim unless the facts establish the

27   defendant's personal involvement in the constitutional deprivation or a causal connection

28   between the defendant's wrongful conduct and the alleged constitutional deprivation.
                                                   8
1    See Hansen v. Black, 885 F.2d 642, 645 (9th Cir. 1989); Johnson v. Duffy, 588 F.2d

2    740, 743-44 (9th Cir. 1978).

3           “In order to establish municipal liability [under Monell], a plaintiff must show that a

4    ‘policy or custom’ led to the plaintiff's injury.” Castro v. County of Los Angeles, 833 F.3d

5    1060, 1073 (9th Cir. 2016) (en banc) (quoting Monell v. Dep't of Soc. Servs. of City of

6    New York, 436 U.S. 658, 694 (1978)). To state a Monell claim, a plaintiff must allege that

7    (1) he was deprived of a constitutional right; (2) the municipality has a policy; (3) the

8    policy amounts to deliberate indifference to plaintiff's constitutional rights; and (4) the

9    policy is the moving force behind the constitutional violation. See Anderson v. Warner,

10   451 F.3d 1063, 1070 (9th Cir. 2006) (citing Oviatt v. Pearce, 954 F.2d 1470, 1474 (9th

11   Cir. 1994)). The complaint must identify the particular policy that the plaintiff alleges

12   caused his constitutional injury. See Lee v. City of Los Angeles, 250 F.3d 668, 681 (9th

13   Cir. 2002); see also Connick v. Thompson, 563 U.S. 51, 60 (2011) (under § 1983, local

14   governments are responsible only for “their own illegal acts,” and therefore to impose

15   liability on a local government, plaintiffs must prove that an “action pursuant to official

16   municipal policy” caused their injury) (citations omitted). Further, the policy at issue must

17   be the result of a decision of a person employed by the entity who has final decision or

18   policymaking authority. Monell, 436 U.S. at 694. There must be a direct causal link

19   between the policy or custom and the injury, and a plaintiff must be able to demonstrate

20   that his injury resulted from a permanent and well-settled practice. Anderson, 451 F.3d

21   at 1070.

22          Here, the SAC fails to state a Section 1983 claim under a Monell theory of liability.

23   Plaintiff alleges his constitutional deprivations were “part of an established custom,

24   policy, and failure of training and supervision within the Glenn County Sheriff’s Office to

25   engage in unlawful detentions, use intimidation tactics, and disregard constitutional

26   rights.” SAC at 3 ¶ 12. Plaintiff alleges that “Chief Gibbs and other supervisory officials

27   within the Glenn County Sheriff’s Office acted . . . within the scope of their employment

28   as policymakers and decision-makers for the County.” Id. at 4 ¶ 2. Plaintiff also alleges
                                                    9
1    that “Chief Gibbs, acting in a supervisory and decision-making capacity, personally

2    directed and oversaw deputies who conducted the unlawful stop, the search, and the

3    confinement.” Id. at 6:11-12. Plaintiff further alleges “the policies, customs, and decisions

4    made and carried out during [Plaintiff’s] arrest and detention were the direct and moving

5    force behind the violations of his constitutional rights.” Id. at 4 ¶ 3. Plaintiff pleads

6    minimal allegations and fails to sufficiently state a Monell claim. As they relate to the

7    “execution of a government’s policy or custom” to deprive constitutional rights, the

8    allegations are conclusory. Monell, 436 U.S. at 694. In his opposition, Plaintiff asserts

9    that “[a]t the pleading stage, a plaintiff is not required to prove the existence of a policy

10   or custom.” To the extent Plaintiff means he does not have to allege non-conclusory

11   facts about a municipal entity’s policy to succeed on a Monell claim, Plaintiff is wrong.

12   See A. E. ex rel. Hernandez v. Cnty. of Tulare, 666 F.3d 631, 637 (9th Cir. 2012)

13   (clarifying that Monell claims may not be pleaded with conclusory allegations reciting

14   elements of cause of action). Thus, the Court recommends that Plaintiff's Monell claims

15   against Defendant Glenn County Sheriff’s Department be dismissed.

16                 3.      Claims Against Defendant Gibbs

17          Plaintiff alleges Defendant Gibbs was present when Plaintiff was pulled over and

18   that Defendant Gibbs shouted, “When are you going to get enough of this woman?

19   Thirty years you’ve been putting up with this woman!” SAC at 3 ¶ 4. Plaintiff also alleges

20   that Defendant Gibbs supervised the actions depriving Plaintiff of his constitutional

21   rights; that Plaintiff’s arrest records were modified, “indicating possible coordination

22   among supervisory personnel—including Chief Gibbs”; and that Defendant Gibbs’s

23   “verbal remarks during the encounter show active participation and bias.” Id. at 4 ¶ 7,

24   5:3-6, 6:11-15. In their motion to dismiss, Defendants argue that to the extent Plaintiff

25   brings claims against Defendant Gibbs, Plaintiff fails to allege sufficient facts to state a

26   claim because (1) claims against Defendant Gibbs in his official capacity must be

27   dismissed as duplicative of Plaintiff’s Monell claim; and (2) any Section 1983 claims

28   against Defendant Gibbs in his individual capacity are conclusorily alleged. Def. Mot. at
                                                    10
1    5-7. The Court agrees.

2           As a threshold matter, the SAC does not indicate whether Plaintiff seeks to bring

3    Section 1983 claims against Gibbs in his individual or official capacity, or whether those

4    allegations are intended to support only Plaintiff’s Monell claim. The Court considers

5    both possibilities. “[A] suit against a state official in his or her official capacity is not a suit

6    against the official but rather is a suit against the official's office. As such, it is no

7    different from a suit against the State itself.” Will v. Mich. Dep't of State Police, 491 U.S.

8    58, 71 (1989) (citation omitted); see Kentucky v. Graham, 473 U.S. 159, 166 (1985). If

9    an individual is sued in his official capacity as a municipal official and the municipal entity

10   is also sued, the claims against the individuals are duplicative and should be dismissed.

11   Coronado v. City of Fresno, 2025 WL 3004080, at *4 (E.D. Cal. Oct. 27, 2025)

12   (dismissing official-capacity claims as duplicative of municipal-liability claims); Myrtle St.

13   Flats LLC v. City of Vallejo, 2017 WL 5754891, at *5 (E.D. Cal. Nov. 28, 2017) (same).

14          Here, a Section 1983 claim against Defendant Gibbs in his official capacity is

15   effectively a Section 1983 claim against Defendant Glenn County Sheriff’s Department.

16   See Coronado, 2025 WL 3004080, at *4. Because Plaintiff also brings claims against the

17   municipal entity (the Glenn County Sheriff’s Department), Plaintiff's claims against

18   Defendant Gibbs should be dismissed as duplicative to the extent they are brought

19   against Defendant Gibbs in his official capacity. See id.; Myrtle St. Flats LLC, 2017 WL

20   5754891, at *5.

21          Even assuming that Plaintiff seeks to bring claims against Defendant Gibbs in his

22   individual capacity, the SAC fails to allege facts sufficient to state a claim. Individual

23   defendants may be held independently liable as supervisors under 42 U.S.C. § 1983 if

24   there exists either “(1) his or her personal involvement in the constitutional deprivation,

25   or (2) a sufficient causal connection between the supervisor's wrongful conduct and the

26   constitutional violation.” Hanson v. Black, 885 F.2d 642, 646 (9th Cir. 1989). “[A]n officer

27   ‘must be more than a mere bystander’ to be liable under § 1983.” Taylor, 2025 WL

28   4059416, at *3 (quoting Reynaga Hernandez v. Skinner, 969 F.3d 930, 941 (9th Cir.
                                                     11
1    2020)). Courts in the Ninth Circuit have dismissed supervisory liability claims against

2    police department chiefs where the pleadings were conclusory and there were no factual

3    allegations to support a causal connection between the chief's conduct and the

4    unconstitutional actions allegedly committed by subordinate officers. See Sterling, 2023

5    WL 2600454, at *6; Mitchell v. County of Contra Costa, 2022 WL 526161, at *7 (N.D.

6    Cal. Feb. 22, 2022) (finding that pleadings that only state that the supervisor “directed

7    his or her subordinates in conduct that violated Plaintiff's rights,” “set in motion a series

8    of acts and omissions by his or her subordinates,” “failed to act to prevent his or her

9    subordinate from engaging in conduct,” and “disregarded the consequence of a known

10   or obvious training deficiency” were conclusory and contained no factual allegations to

11   support supervisory liability); Arteaga v. City of Oakley, 2020 WL 511876, at *7 (N.D.

12   Cal. Jan. 31, 2020) (dismissing a claim for supervisory liability on similarly pleaded

13   allegations).

14          Here, Plaintiff’s SAC falls short in similar ways. The SAC’s allegations do not

15   sufficiently allege that Defendant Gibbs personally participated in constitutional violations

16   against Plaintiff or that Defendant Gibbs’s conduct had a causal connection to

17   unconstitutional actions allegedly committed by subordinate officers. However, Plaintiff’s

18   opposition alleges for the first time that when Plaintiff was stopped, Defendant Gibbs

19   “announced that Plaintiff was under arrest for domestic violence.” Pl. Opp’n at 5. This

20   allegation is unavailing. On a motion to dismiss under Rule 12(b)(6), “a court may not

21   look beyond the complaint to a plaintiff's moving papers, such as a memorandum in

22   opposition to a defendant's motion to dismiss.” Schneider v. California Dep't of Corr., 151

23   F.3d 1194, 1197 n.1 (9th Cir. 1998) (emphasis in original). The Court disregards

24   Plaintiff’s allegations raised for the first time in his opposition. However, even if the Court

25   were to consider this allegation, Plaintiff has not sufficiently pleaded Defendant Gibbs’s

26   personal participation in an unlawful arrest with factual allegations that would indicate a

27   lack of probable cause. See Graves v. Fresno Cnty. Mental Health, 2019 WL 2125457

28   (E.D. Cal. May 15, 2019) (holding that general allegations of lack of probable cause for
                                                   12
1    arrest are insufficient to state claim under Section 1983).

2           Accordingly, Plaintiff has failed to plead liability against Defendant Gibbs in his

3    individual capacity. The Court therefore recommends dismissal of any claims Plaintiff

4    brings against Defendant Gibbs.

5           C.     Leave to Amend

6           Although the Federal Rules adopt a flexible pleading policy, even a pro se

7    litigant's complaint must give fair notice and state the elements of a claim plainly and

8    succinctly. Jones v. Community Redev. Agency, 733 F.2d 646, 649 (9th Cir. 1984). If the

9    court finds that a complaint should be dismissed for failure to state a claim, it has

10   discretion to dismiss with or without leave to amend. Lopez, 203 F.3d at 1126-30. Leave

11   to amend should be granted if it appears possible that the defects in the complaint could

12   be corrected, especially if a plaintiff is pro se. Id. at 1130-31; see also Cato, 70 F.3d at

13   1106 (“A pro se litigant must be given leave to amend his or her complaint, and some

14   notice of its deficiencies, unless it is absolutely clear that the deficiencies of the

15   complaint could not be cured by amendment.”) (citation omitted). However, if, after

16   careful consideration, it is clear that a complaint cannot be cured by amendment, the

17   court may dismiss without leave to amend. Cato, 70 F.3d at 1105-06.

18          Here, Plaintiff has amended his complaint twice. (See ECF Nos. 17, 30.) The

19   Court also provided Plaintiff a detailed explanation of the deficiencies in his First

20   Amended Complaint. See 10/14/2025 Order and Findings and Recommendations.

21   Despite this, Plaintiff has been unable to cure the deficiencies and allege sufficient facts

22   to state a claim against any defendant. Accordingly, the Court finds that further

23   amendment would be futile. The SAC should therefore be dismissed without leave to

24   amend. See Lopez, 203 F.3d at 1130-31; Cato, 70 F.3d at 1105-06.
