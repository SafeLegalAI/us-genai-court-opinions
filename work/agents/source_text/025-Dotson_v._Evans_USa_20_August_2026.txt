II.

Legal Standards
A.

Ex Parte Applications

Ex parte applications are solely for extraordinary relief and are rarely granted. See
Mission Power Eng’g Co. v. Cont’l Cas. Co., 883 F. Supp. 488, 492 (C.D. Cal. 1995); Dkt.
# 12, Standing Order, ¶ 11 (“Ex parte applications are solely for extraordinary relief, are
rarely granted, and should be used with discretion.”). In order to justify ex parte relief, the
moving party must establish: (1) that its “cause will be irreparably prejudiced if the
underlying motion is heard according to regular noticed motion procedures[;]” and (2) that
it “is without fault in creating the crisis that requires ex parte relief, or that the crisis
occurred as a result of excusable neglect.” Mission Power, 883 F. Supp. at 492. “To show
irreparable prejudice, it will usually be necessary to refer to the merits of the accompanying
proposed motion, because if it is meritless, failure to hear it cannot be prejudicial.” Id. “To
show that the moving party is without fault, or guilty only of excusable neglect, requires
more than a showing that the other party is the sole wrongdoer. It is the creation of the
crisis—the necessity for bypassing regular motion procedures—that requires explanation.”
Id. at 493.
B.

Rule 16

A scheduling order “controls the course of the action unless the court modifies it.”
Fed. R. Civ. P. 16(d). Once a district court enters a scheduling order, “[a] schedule may
be modified only for good cause and with the judge’s consent.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 16(b)(4);
see also Hartzell v. Marana Unified Sch. Dist., 130 F.4th 722, 745 (9th Cir. 2025);
Thompson v. BMW of N. Am., 334 F.R.D. 230, 223 (C.D. Cal. 2020); C.D. Cal. L.R. 16-14.
District courts must enter scheduling orders in actions to “limit the time to join other
parties, amend the pleadings, complete discovery, and file motions.” Id. at 16(b)(3). Once
entered by the court, a scheduling order “controls the course of the action unless the court

problems. Johnson v. Mammoth Recreations, Inc., 975 F.2d 604, 610 (9th Cir. 1992). As
such, a scheduling order is “the heart of case management.” Koplove v. Ford Motor Co.,
795 F.2d 15, 18 (3rd Cir. 1986). In the Ninth Circuit, “Rule 16(b)’s ‘good cause’ standard
primarily considers the diligence of the party seeking the amendment.” Johnson, 975 F.2d
at 609. In other words, if the scheduling order could not have been met despite the
diligence of the party seeking the extension, modification may be appropriate. See id. If
the party was not diligent, however, the inquiry ends. See id.
It is well-established that the “good cause” standard “primarily considers the
diligence of the party seeking” modification of the Court’s scheduling order, and “if that
party was not diligent, the inquiry should end.” Hartzell, 130 F.4th at 745 (cleaned up);
see also Thurber v. Costco Wholesale Corp., No. 5:25-cv-00005-DTB, 2025 WL 4058320,
at *2 (C.D. Cal. Dec. 30, 2025) (“While no one factor is necessarily dispositive, the Ninth
Circuit has instructed that the primary focus should be on whether the party seeking to
reopen discovery has acted diligently.”) (internal quotation marks and citation omitted);
“Relevant inquiries into diligence include: whether the movant was diligent in helping the
court to create a workable Rule 16 order; whether matters that were not, and could not have
been, foreseeable at the time of the scheduling conference caused the need for amendment;
and whether the movant was diligent in seeking amendment once the need to amend
became apparent.” United States ex rel. Terry v. Wasatch Advantage Grp., LLC, 327
F.R.D. 395, 404 (E.D. Cal. 2018) (cleaned up); Tisdale v. Apria Healthcare LLC, No.
2:24-cv-09620-AH (PYCx), 2025 WL 1674419, at *3 (C.D. Cal. May 22, 2025) (same).
III.

Discussion

To satisfy the first Mission Power factor, Plaintiff must show that his cause will
be irreparably prejudiced if the underlying motion is heard according to regular noticed
procedures. Mission Power, 883 F. Supp. at 492. If the Court were to evaluate a motion
to modify the scheduling order under regular noticed procedures, the earliest the Court
would have all briefing would be September 18, 2026, after the August 26 deadline to file
a motion for class certification would have passed. See L.R. 6-1. Accordingly, the Court
finds that Plaintiff’s cause would be irreparably prejudiced if the underlying motion to
modify the scheduling order is heard according to regular noticed procedures.

Turning to the second Mission Power factor, Plaintiff must show that he is without
fault in creating the crisis that requires ex parte relief, or that the crisis occurred due to
excusable neglect. See Mission Power, 883 F. Supp. at 492. Plaintiff must further show
that he was diligent in obtaining discovery in preparation to file a motion for class
certification, and that he was diligent in seeking amendment once the need to amend
became apparent. Plaintiff’s Application fails this factor on all such grounds.
The Application focuses on Plaintiff’s inability to file a motion for class
certification without the benefit of full responses to certain outstanding discovery requests
and prior to taking any depositions. See generally Appl.; Friedman Decl. ISO Appl.
Plaintiff states that the parties have cooperated and diligently engaged in discovery, but
places blame on the “practical realities of the volume of [ESI] involved and the time
reasonably required to complete the ESI protocol.” Appl. at 11. While there very well may
be a large volume of ESI to sift through, Plaintiff provides no explanation for why he did
not seek to prioritize discovery related to the narrower issue of class certification. Instead,
Plaintiff offers generalized statements that the outstanding discovery must be completed
before filing for class certification and offers the Court only vague information regarding
the substance and importance of outstanding discovery. See, e.g., Friedman Decl. ISO
Appl. ¶ 5 (“Plaintiff cannot demonstrate the elements required for class
certification . . . without the customer, sales, and product data that Defendant has not yet
produced.”); see also Appl. at 10–12 (failing to specify contents and basis for necessity of
outstanding discovery). But Plaintiff’s broad assertions that the outstanding discovery is
“necessary” do not satisfy Mission Power. See, e.g., Garcia v. Dolex Dollar Express, Inc.,
No. 223CV02013MEMFAS, 2023 WL 6170339, at *1 (C.D. Cal. Aug. 24, 2023) (denying
ex parte application to extend class certification deadline to complete outstanding
discovery where “[Plaintiff] has not set forth any reason why the supplemental responses
that are still outstanding have any bearing on the motion for class certification. Even
assuming the discovery is relevant to class certification, the timing of events does not
support ex parte relief here.”).
The Court finds that, despite Plaintiff’s counsel’s “extensive experience as
appointed class counsel and in litigating contested motions for class certification,” Plaintiff
has not demonstrated his own diligence in pursuing the discovery necessary to support a
motion for class certification. Friedman Decl. ISO Appl. ¶ 10; see Millsaps v. Pinal Cnty.
Sup. Ct., 494 Fed. Appx. 821, 823 (9th Cir. 2012) (stating that the party moving to modify
the scheduling order has the burden of showing diligence); Yang v. ActioNet, Inc., No. CV
14-00792-AB (SHx), 2015 WL 13385917, at *2 (C.D. Cal. July 21, 2025) (same).

Indeed, Plaintiff’s realization at the eleventh hour that he may have squandered his
ability to certify a class does not constitute the “diligence” or “excusable neglect” provided
for by Mission Power. 883 F. Supp. at 493. See, e.g., Netlist Inc. v. Samsung Elecs. Co.,
No. 8:20-CV-00993-MCS-ADS, 2021 WL 12282030, at *1 (C.D. Cal. Aug. 3, 2021)
(denying ex parte application where movant had “ample time to present a regularly noticed
motion” and now presents an application which “effectively asks the Court to condone its
strategic decision to set fact depositions to occur shortly after Samsung's deadline to
produce documents. . . . This is not an appropriate reason for emergency relief.”); Netlist
Inc. v. Samsung Elecs. Co., No. 8:20-CV-00993-MCS-DFM, 2021 WL 6103541, at *1
(C.D. Cal. July 7, 2021) (denying ex parte application where “Defendant made the strategic
decision to focus its efforts on an informal resolution instead of taking discovery in the past
four months. . . . It did so at its own peril.”). Plaintiff further fails to establish his diligence
in pursuing an amendment to the scheduling order, which was issued over six months prior
to the class certification deadline, and six months before Plaintiff filed the instant
Application. See Mission Power, 883 F. Supp. at 488 (“[M]erely showing that trial is fast
approaching and that the opposing party still has not answered crucial interrogatories is
insufficient. . . .”).
Plaintiff contends that ABS Entertainment, Inc. v. CBS Corp., 908 F.3d 405 (9th Cir.
2018) is “controlling” in its holding that “a district court abuses its discretion when it denies
a stipulated extension of a class certification deadline without addressing the parties’ stated
need for class certification discovery . . . .” Appl. at 12. Plaintiff relies on ABS
Entertainment to argue that this Court abused its discretion in denying Plaintiff’s
Stipulation “without addressing the Parties’ asserted, undisputed need for the outstanding
discovery.” Id. at 13. Plaintiff misconstrues the holding of that case. The Ninth Circuit
subsequently clarified its holding in ABS Entertainment: “a district court may abuse its
discretion if it unreasonably applies rules in a way that deprives a party of an opportunity
to present class allegations or a motion for class certification.” Davidson v. O'Reilly Auto
Enters., LLC, 968 F.3d 955, 963 (9th Cir. 2020). Indeed, the “abuse of discretion” found
in ABS Entertainment was the court’s “striking the timely filed motion [for class
certification] as a sanction for what it categorizes as ‘trivial’ omissions in the notice.” ABS
Ent., Inc. v. CBS Corp., 908 F.3d 405, 427 (9th Cir. 2018); see also Davidson, 968 F.3d at
964 (“[In ABS Entertainment] we agreed that the district court ‘abused its discretion in
striking the timely filed motion as a sanction for trivial omissions in the notice,’ and we
reversed and remanded so the district court could consider the class-certification motion
on the merits, along with whether pre-certification discovery was warranted.”)

IV.

OSC Regarding Case Citations

At three different points in his Application, Plaintiff attributes quoted language to
Mission Power, 883 F. Supp. at 492, which appears nowhere in the body of the cited case.
See Appl. at 7:10–7:15, 10:5–10:8, 10:17–10:22. The discrepancies between the quoted
language and the language of the cited case are so significant as to abrogate any assumption
that the misattributions are the result of typographical error.
Plaintiff’s repeated inclusion of quotations misattributed to Mission Power, which
are present in the language of an entirely different case, 1 “bear the hallmarks of
hallucinated cases created by artificial intelligence (‘AI’) tools.” Cummins v. Becerra, No.
1:25-CV-01853-DC-AC, 2026 WL 373336, at *1 (E.D. Cal. Feb. 10, 2026) (issuing order
to show cause for AI hallucinated case citation, including citations where cases existed but
cited language did not exist); see also Mata v. Avianca, Inc., 678 F. Supp. 3d 443, 454
(S.D.N.Y. 2023) (sanctioning attorney for AI hallucinated citations, including to cases
which “have correct names and citations but do not contain the language quoted or support
the propositions for which they are offered.” ).
