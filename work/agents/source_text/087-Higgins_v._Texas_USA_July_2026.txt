                                   MEMORANDUM OPINION

       Kevin Dale Higgins appeals his conviction for directing the activities of a street gang. In
two issues, Appellant challenges the sufficiency of the evidence and contends trial counsel
provided ineffective assistance during guilt-innocence and punishment. We affirm.


                                           BACKGROUND
       Appellant was charged by indictment with directing the activities of a criminal street gang,
i.e., as part of the identifiable leadership of a criminal street gang, Appellant “knowingly financed,
directed, and supervised members of his criminal street gang to conspire to commit and to commit”
aggravated assault with a deadly weapon and murder. Appellant pleaded “not guilty,” and the
matter proceeded to a jury trial in December 2024. The trial court declared a mistrial after the jury
indicated that it was deadlocked at 11-1 in favor of conviction.
       A new jury trial began on January 13, 2025. Special Agent Doug Pearson of the Colorado
Bureau of Investigation testified that “outlaw motorcycle gang culture is developed around strict
protocols, strict rules of engagement, strictly monitored by a chain of command and hierarchy and
expectations of behavior. And it’s all based around respect and violence.” Pearson explained that
the national president of an outlaw motorcycle gang possesses “absolute authority” and “dictates
everything that goes on” in the organization. According to Pearson, “there’s nothing that goes on
in the organization that [the] national president does not approve, does not order, does not dictate,
does not have 100 percent control of anything that occurs. And that’s all done through a very,
very strict chain of command all the way from the national president . . . down to the very lowest
members.” Pearson explained that the national vice president is under the president and primarily
responsible for filling in for the president if the president is unavailable. In such an instance, the
president’s totalitarian power shifts to the vice president. The national sergeant at arms is
responsible for the national president’s safety and security, as well as “handing down any orders
from the national president, down the chain of command, as it relates to protocols, violence,
expectations. He is the conduit to other sergeants at arms[.]” Pearson stated that the national
sergeant at arms passes orders down to the nomad sergeants at arms, the regional sergeants at arms,
and the chapter sergeants at arms, and those orders are then passed along to rank and file gang
members.
         Pearson explained that sometime before 2015, the Ugly Man Cossacks (UMC) and the
Bandidos, both motorcycle gangs, brokered a deal, pursuant to which the Bandidos allowed the
Cossacks to display a Texas bottom rocker. 1 Marshall Mitchell, the Bandidos “nomads president”
and a longtime Bandidos member, and Appellant, a UMC member and UMC’s vice president at
that time, brokered the deal. Because rank and file members of the Bandidos were not informed
that UMC had permission to display the rocker, many Bandidos became upset upon seeing UMC
members doing so. As a result, numerous violent events occurred between the Bandidos and UMC,
which resulted in a meeting between the two clubs to resolve the issue in May 2015 in Waco,
Texas. Violence immediately broke out at the Waco meeting, and seven members of UMC and
two members of the Bandidos were killed. Before the Waco incident, numerous messages from
UMC’s leadership instructed members to “bring as many as you can” and “bring your tools” (i.e.
“bring weapons”) to the meeting.



         1
           Pearson explained that a cut (i.e. motorcycle vest) has three primary features: (1) a top rocker that identifies
the name of the gang or club, (2) a center patch that displays a mascot or symbol that is unique to that club or gang,
and (3) a bottom rocker that displays the name of a state that the gang or club claims as its territory.




                                                            2
       After the Waco incident, UMC split into two clubs: the original Cossacks (UMC) and the
1% Cossacks. Pearson explained that the 1%er Cossacks started a new club but retained the
Cossacks’ name and symbols, and the new club’s existence disrespected UMC and violated
UMC’s bylaws. According to Pearson, Appellant ultimately became national president of UMC,
and Pearson described Appellant as “the top guy.” Pearson testified, “Mr. Higgins was the national
president of the Cossacks. My understanding, based on what I know about this investigation[,] is
he was directing violence.”
       Captain Jason Railsback of the Smith County Sheriff’s office testified that on May 2, 2020,
Brandon Edwards, a member of the 1% Cossacks and its sergeant at arms, was shot and killed
while riding his motorcycle. Railsback learned that a witness heard Edwards’s bike traveling the
roadway while being closely followed by a speeding pickup truck. The witness saw two white
males “hanging out of the passenger side windows[,]” heard gunfire, and saw Edwards’s bike fall.
Another witness, the president of the Rose City 1%ers, told officers, “the only club that we’re
having problems with right now is the Ugly Man Cossacks.” Said witness indicated that UMC’s
regional sergeant at arms, Jose Valenzuela, drove a truck that matched the description of the truck
seen following Edwards. Officers subsequently stopped Valenzuela’s vehicle for a traffic violation
and detained Valenzuela and the other occupants of the vehicle. Officers ultimately learned that
two other members of UMC, Joshua Tibbits and Jeffrey Griffin, were in the truck with Valenzuela
when Edwards was shot. Valenzuela told officers that the murder weapons were sawed into pieces
and placed in a bucket, which was filled with concrete and ultimately thrown into the Sabine River.
A dive team later recovered the murder weapons.
       During the investigation, officers obtained the cell phones of Barcelona, Griffin, and
Tibbits. According to Railsback, although Valenzuela did not directly contact Appellant, the
manner in which orders were given and information was relayed in UMC fits with both the call
logs and UMC’s chain of command. Railsback agreed that all the evidence points to orders coming
from Appellant. Detective Timothy “T.J.” Cutright, who assisted with the investigation of
Edwards’s murder, testified that Appellant was “in a position of national leadership” and “the top
of the chain” when the murder occurred. Cutright explained that he saw no evidence of phone
calls, text messages, or emails from Appellant, but UMC operates via a chain of command,
meaning that “every order is authorized by the person above, which ultimately ends with
[Appellant], or begins with him, essentially.”



                                                 3
       Investigating officers learned that during the months before Edwards’s murder, members
of UMC hunted members of the 1%ers, committed “numerous assaults and robberies[,]” and
attempted to destroy evidence. Railsback explained that there was “absolutely a directive” that “if
they come across a member of the 1% Cossacks, not only would they be allowed to assault them,
they are expected to assault them.” Said directive was known as a “smash-on-sight” (SOS) order.
Officers learned that UMC’s national sergeant at arms received a direct SOS order from Appellant,
the national president of UMC, and the national sergeant at arms passed on the order to local
chapters. UMC members who chose not to engage members of the 1% Cossacks would be
punished by their sergeant at arms.
       The State offered into evidence various personal items of UMC memorabilia obtained from
Valenzuela’s residence, to which defense counsel objected on relevancy grounds and under Texas
Rule of Evidence 403.      Outside the jury’s presence, the trial judge referred to a written
memorandum defense counsel filed regarding the admissibility of said items. During the ensuing
colloquy between the trial judge and defense counsel, the trial judge stated that defense counsel’s
memorandum misstated the holdings of several cases, whereupon defense counsel admitted that
the memorandum was produced by artificial intelligence and he did not read the cases cited therein.
The trial judge overruled defense counsel’s objection and admitted the items into evidence. The
trial court admitted additional memorabilia from Valenzuela’s home into evidence over defense
counsel’s objections.
       According to Railsback, UMC consists of three or more persons having a common
identifying sign, symbol, or identifying leadership who continuously or regularly associate in the
commission of criminal offenses. Railsback testified that he heard “numerous times” from UMC
members that Appellant became the national president of UMC after overthrowing his predecessor
by calling for a “directed vote,” meaning that “members were told who they’re voting for.” UMC
members also told Railsback that Appellant pushed UMC to become more violent. Railsback
explained, “[t]hrough our investigation, we learned that [Appellant] had given . . . instructions to
have every sergeant at arms given a specific task on a specific member of the 1%er clubs that they
were to go assault or put down.” Railsback stated that Appellant was UMC’s registered agent on
the Secretary of State’s website.
       Special Agent Clay Steelman of the Texas Department of Public Safety’s criminal
investigations division testified that when the 1% Cossacks formed from UMC, but retained their



                                                 4
name and color scheme, “that is a sign of ultimate disrespect to the original club.” Steelman
described Appellant’s official ascent to the office of UMC’s national president in March 2020, as
well as Appellant’s status as acting president then due to his predecessor’s unavailability. At the
behest of the Smith County Sheriff’s Office, Steelman became involved in the investigation after
Edwards’s murder in May 2020. Steelman learned that Edwards wore a 1%er Cossack cut at the
time of his murder. Steelman led the portion of the investigation related to organized criminal
activity, and Railsback led the murder investigation. Steelman explained, “[d]uring the course of
my investigation, I was able to uncover numerous incidents . . . throughout the state that indicated
to me that this murder that occurred on May 2nd of 2020 was not a singular event but rather the
culmination of many events[.]” He opined that Edwards’s death resulted from a green light or
smash-on-sight order from national leadership to UMC members. During his investigation,
Steelman learned that Tibbits, Griffin, and Crowell committed a surveillance operation with the
intent to shoot and kill Owen Reeves (“Big O”), a former UMC member and the founder of the
1% Cossacks.
       During his investigation, Steelman interviewed Crowell, Valenzuela, Tibbits, and Griffin.
Valenzuela ultimately became a cooperating witness and provided detailed information regarding
the location of the murder weapons. Steelman began to focus on the club and its leadership.
According to Steelman, Appellant was the de facto head of UMC from approximately 2015 to
2019 and “played a vital role with the organization.” Steelman described Appellant’s role in
communicating with the Bandidos before the Waco incident, as well as the club’s subsequent split.
       According to Steelman, in January 2020, a conflict between a 1% Cossack and a member
of UMC occurred at a motorcycle shop. The conflict consisted of “a verbal argument” and possibly
pushing and shoving. The UMC member involved in said altercation called other members for
help, and Tibbits, Griffin, and another man “showed up and followed the 1%er.” When the 1%
member reached the clubhouse, Tibbits, Griffin, and the third man “struck him with their truck[.]”
In February 2020, multiple UMC members went to the 1%ers’ clubhouse in Gilmer and “began
looking for individuals to commit assaults.” Steelman explained that Tibbits, Griffin, and a third
UMC member committed “an aggravated assault with brass knuckles against two members of the
1% Cossacks.” Shortly thereafter, “three more individuals in rural Gilmer were able to locate
another 1% Cossack” at a gas station and committed “a stabbing/aggravated robbery” during which
they took the victim’s 1% Cossack cut. The following month, Valenzuela and Tibbits shot Billy



                                                 5
Couch, a member of the 1% Cossacks, and Edwards’s murder occurred on May 2, 2020. According
to Steelman, call logs establish that Crowell was in contact with Valenzuela.
       Steelman learned that after the club split, UMC members surveilled 1% Cossacks members
on social media and examined the 1% Cossacks’ website in an effort “to find out where they were
going and who was going to be there.” Steelman explained that Valenzuela used his social media
page to surveil the national sergeant at arms of the 1% Cossacks in Gilmer, and after the Gilmer
incident, Valenzuela was promoted to regional sergeant at arms. According to Steelman, given
“the progression of violence that was occurring, coupled with the promotions that were
occurring[,]” Cossacks’ national leadership knew about violence in which members engaged.
Steelman described Appellant’s position as national president as “essentially absolute” and “a
very, very powerful position.” In addition, Steelman explained that the national president selects
the national sergeant at arms, and if Appellant disapproved of the level of violence in which UMC
members engaged, he could have replaced Crowell as national sergeant at arms. According to
Steelman, the men that Appellant and Crowell promoted to sergeants at arms shared a propensity
to commit violence. Steelman obtained a copy of the minutes from a west Texas regional UMC
meeting and saw an entry therein that stated, “Smash-on-sight, no matter what.” When asked
whether there is “any way at all that a western region hears about a smash-on-sight, writes it down
in their minutes, and the national president is totally unaware[]”, Steelman responded, “No, sir.”
According to Steelman, when he informed Appellant that a warrant was issued for his arrest,
Appellant did not surrender until approximately ten days later.
       Griffin testified that he (1) was previously a member of UMC, (2) pleaded “guilty” to
engaging in organized criminal activity with the predicate offense of murder and is incarcerated
for that offense, (3) was present at the murder of Edwards, and (4) decided to cooperate with the
State after learning that Valenzuela did so. In 2018, Griffin became his chapter’s sergeant at arms,
and he described his role as the chapter’s police force, security team, and enforcers. Griffin
explained that the existence of the 1% Cossacks greatly disrespected UMC, and he referred to
members of the 1% Cossacks as “counterfeits.” Crowell informed Griffin that UMC’s leadership
decided to escalate the situation with the 1% Cossacks. Griffin explained that Crowell said, “we’ve
got the green light. . . . It’s a smash-on-sight.” According to Griffin, Crowell further stated, “If
you’re a sergeant at arms . . . and I hear about you not smashing on one of them . . . when you see
them, then you’re going to answer to me.” Griffin assumed that the green light came from national



                                                 6
officers pursuant to UMC’s chain of command. As a result of the order, Griffin performed “recon
missions” by searching social media for the license plate numbers of members of the 1% Cossacks.
Griffin provided any license plate numbers that he found to his regional sergeant at arms, Tibbits,
whose superior officer was the national sergeant at arms.
       According to Griffin, Tibbits ordered UMC members to strike a counterfeiters’ Halloween
party near Waco, and they did so. Griffin explained that such an order would have come to Tibbits
from the national chapter. In 2019, UMC’s president was unavailable due to his job as the captain
of a ship, so Appellant assumed the authority of president. Griffin believed that Appellant made
command and control decisions for UMC at the time of the smash-on-sight order. Griffin
described incidents in which he and other UMC members ran over a 1% Cossack who robbed a
UMC member and assaulted someone who “talked bad about the club.” In January and February
of 2020, UMC members were ordered to focus on the 1% Cossacks’ clubhouse in Gilmer, and
both the resulting strike and the previous strike in October 2019 involved multiple UMC regions
and chapters. Griffin testified that the order did not come solely from Crowell; rather, UMC’s
national officers knew of the strikes. During the Gilmer strike, Griffin wore brass knuckles, and
he and others assaulted 1%ers at a convenience store.
       In May 2020, when Appellant was national president of UMC, Tibbits instructed Griffin
to return to East Texas to serve as “backup.” Griffin explained that although Appellant acted as
president before that date, Appellant officially became national president after a vote that was
“pretty much a hostile takeover.” Some of UMC’s members disagreed with Appellant’s ascent to
president by pushing out the previous president and decided to “do a lay down,” meaning they
would give up their Cossacks patches. Griffin claimed Tibbits summoned him because he
anticipated that the lay down might result in violence.
       Griffin explained that he and Tibbits went to Valenzuela’s home, and the group eventually
got into Valenzuela’s truck. Tibbits was armed with a pistol, and Griffin’s pistol was in
Valenzuela’s possession. While the three drove, a “counterfeit” (i.e. Edwards) pulled up next to
them, and they decided to “take care of” the situation. According to Griffin, Valenzuela
accelerated, handed Griffin the pistol, and ordered Griffin, “Smoke him.” Valenzuela pulled closer
to Edwards, and Griffin testified that both he and Tibbits rolled down their windows, leaned
outside, and fired at Edwards. Griffin saw Edwards slump over the handlebars of his motorcycle,
and he continued firing at Edwards as they passed him. Griffin testified, “as we passed him, I



                                                 7
look[ed] in the back window, and then that’s when I s[aw] him veer off the road and go down and
wreck.” Later that evening, Griffin learned that Edwards died. Griffin, Valenzuela, and Tibbits
burned their clothing, cut the guns into pieces, placed the pieces in a bucket, and filled the bucket
with concrete. On their way back to Waco, Griffin and Tibbits met in person with Crowell, who
was “excited” and offered to reimburse them for the pistols. During cross-examination, Griffin
testified that Appellant did not tell him to hit anyone with brass knuckles, put anyone in the
hospital, or murder anyone.
       Valenzuela testified that he was a member of UMC, a regional sergeant at arms, and
pleaded “guilty” to murdering Edwards as part of a criminal street gang. According to Valenzuela,
Appellant took over his predecessor’s responsibilities in the fall of 2019, and Appellant officially
became the Cossacks’ national president in March 2020. Valenzuela explained UMC’s’ issues
with the 1% Cossacks, as well as their efforts to find 1%ers and the occurrence of fights and
shootouts between the two groups. According to Valenzuela, national officers, including Appellant
and Crowell, instructed him and other UMC members to take Cossacks patches from members of
1% Cossacks. Valenzuela explained that “smash-on-sight” meant “[w]hen you see them, smash
them[,]” and said order came from above. According to Valenzuela, Appellant informed Crowell
that he “wanted to see more action” regarding the smash-on-sight order. Valenzuela described his
participation in the occurrence at Gilmer. Valenzuela also described his participation in the “lay
down” incident that culminated in the murder of Edwards, as well as disposing of the murder
weapons. Valenzuela agreed that Appellant never instructed him to assault or murder anyone.
       Tibbits testified that he was a member of UMC and is currently incarcerated for engaging
in organized criminal activity with the predicate offense of murder. Tibbits served as sergeant at
arms for the UMC chapter in Waco and eventually became a regional sergeant at arms. Because
of UMC’s dispute with the 1% Cossacks, Tibbits hunted 1%ers, and he assaulted any that he
encountered. Tibbits testified that he never heard Appellant instruct anyone to harm or kill anyone.
According to Tibbits, his instructions came from Crowell. Tibbits admitted that when authorities
interviewed him, he said it was impossible that Appellant did not know about what was going on,
but at trial, Tibbits claimed that he lied during the interview.
       Crowell testified that he was a member of UMC from approximately 2016 until 2020, and
he served as a chapter sergeant at arms and later became a regional sergeant at arms. In addition,
Crowell acted as national sergeant at arms during the national sergeant at arms’ incapacitation.



                                                  8
Crowell explained that the State charged him with engaging in organized criminal activity, but he
reached no agreement with the State in exchange for his testimony at Appellant’s trial. According
to Crowell, although Appellant was not the UMC national president in 2019, Appellant organized
talks in an attempt to resolve issues between UMC and the 1% Cossacks because of the president’s
unavailability. In addition, Crowell testified that Appellant acted as national president in 2016,
when UMC experienced issues with the Bandidos. Appellant sent Crowell and UMC members on
a “hunting trip,” during which they engaged in several fights over a two-day period.
       While Crowell acted as national sergeant at arms, Appellant called him regarding issues
with another motorcycle club in Louisiana, instructed him to “get together some guys and go down
there and take care of it[,]” and told Crowell that it was an opportunity for Crowell to prove
himself. Crowell contacted regional and chapter sergeants at arms and traveled to Louisiana with
five other sergeants at arms. After their arrival in Louisiana, an intense fight broke out, during
which brass knuckles were employed, a man was stabbed, and someone drew a gun. When
Crowell contacted Appellant after the fight, Appellant said, “Good job[,]” and that event cemented
Crowell’s status as national sergeant at arms. According to Crowell, “nothing went on without
[Appellant] knowing about it.” Crowell agreed that national officers give orders to regional
officers, regional officers give orders to chapter officers, and individual chapters give orders to
“rank and file guys.” Crowell explained that Appellant instructed him and other regional sergeants
at arms to acquire and use burner phones to avoid law enforcement.
       According to Crowell, during a national meeting, Appellant and UMC’s then-president
decided to escalate the situation with the 1% Cossacks, and Appellant said, “there’s a green
light[,]” meaning that anytime a Cossack encountered a 1%er, they would fight. Upon receiving
the green light, Crowell contacted regional officers and asked them to conduct reconnaissance of
1% Cossacks members. Appellant told Crowell that he planned to kill a 1%er known as Big O by
shooting him at his home with a sniper rifle. Crowell testified that he and Tibbits conducted
reconnaissance of Big O for two days while armed with ARs, sniper rifles, and pistols, but Big O
never exited his home. On one occasion when Crowell was in a truck with Valenzuela, Appellant
called Crowell and stated that he was upset because no one was “doing anything about the 1%ers.”
In February 2020, Crowell and four other UMC members conducted an organized “hunting
mission” that ultimately led to an altercation with the 1% Cossacks. After said altercation, Crowell
promoted Valenzuela to regional sergeant at arms.

       According to Crowell, Appellant was national president of UMC at the time of Edwards’
murder. After the murder, Appellant called Crowell and asked, “do you know if this is our
people?” and “[d]o we have anything to do with this?” Crowell promised to find out and report
back. Valenzuela, Tibbits, and Griffin asked to meet with Crowell and informed Crowell that the

       During cross-examination, defense counsel sought to play portions of Crowell’s interview

denied defense counsel’s request to play the recording and stated, “There’s not been an

just not proper impeachment.” When the jury returned to the courtroom, Crowell testified that it
was Appellant’s idea to travel to Waco to kill Big O, and he gave a recorded statement to that
effect. Crowell explained that Appellant’s predecessor gave a general “green light,” and wanted
to “handle Big O[,]” but it was Appellant’s idea “to go to Waco and do it the way [Appellant]
wanted to do it.”
       While defense counsel continued cross-examining Crowell, audio played on defense

       Kenneth Baker testified that he has known Valenzuela for a long time. Baker explained

       During deliberations, the jury sent a note to the court that asked, “Who is going to protect

with defense counsel, the trial judge polled the jury by asking that each juror who joined in the

verdict to raise their hands, and all of the jurors raised their hands. After hearing the punishment

        Appellant obtained new counsel for appeal and filed a motion for trial, in which he
contended that trial counsel provided ineffective assistance. On March 27, 2025, the trial court
conducted an evidentiary hearing on Appellant’s motion. The trial court’s bailiff, Deputy Brittnee

deliberating, jurors expressed concern about “a couple of people that were out in the hallway.”
Young explained that she informed the trial court of the jurors’ concern and defense counsel was
made aware of the concern. When the jury sent a note during deliberations, the trial court

According to Young, there were people in the courtroom, including Appellant’s family, who
appeared to be part of a motorcycle club. Young recalled seeing two such individuals who were
not members of Appellant’s family and explained that they were present throughout the trial.
        Appellant testified that he learned of his arrest warrant on April 10, 2024, while he was out
of the state working. He retained defense counsel and informed counsel of the arrest warrant.
Appellant explained that defense counsel, who was out of the country at the time, advised him to

                                         EVIDENTIARY SUFFICIENCY
         In issue one, Appellant challenges the sufficiency of the evidence to support the verdict.
Specifically, Appellant asserts that, absent evidence from accomplice witnesses, the evidence is
insufficient.
Standard of Review and Applicable Law
         When evaluating the sufficiency of the evidence, we review all the evidence in the light
most favorable to the verdict to determine whether any rational factfinder could have found the
essential elements of the offense beyond a reasonable doubt. Brooks v. State, 323 S.W.3d 893,
902 n. 19 (Tex. Crim. App. 2010) (citing Jackson v. Virginia, 443 U.S. 307, 319, 99 S. Ct. 2781,
2789, 61 L. Ed. 2d 560 (1979)); Hooper v. State, 214 S.W.3d 9, 13 (Tex. Crim. App. 2007). The
jury is the exclusive judge of the credibility of witnesses and the weight to be given to their
testimony. Brooks, 323 S.W.3d at 899; Penagraph v. State, 623 S.W.2d 341, 343 (Tex. Crim.
App. [Panel Op.] 1981); see also Jackson, 443 U.S. at 319, 99 S. Ct. at 2789. The factfinder may
accept one version of the facts and reject another or reject any of a witness’s testimony.
Penagraph, 623 S.W.2d at 343.
         A reviewing court must give full deference to the factfinder’s responsibility to fairly
resolve conflicts in the testimony, to weigh the evidence, and to draw reasonable inferences from
basic facts to ultimate facts. Hooper, 214 S.W.3d at 13. If the record contains conflicting
inferences, we must presume that the factfinder resolved such facts in favor of the verdict and defer
to that resolution. Brooks, 323 S.W.3d at 899 n.13; Clayton v. State, 235 S.W.3d 772, 778 (Tex.
Crim. App. 2007). In addition, we determine whether the necessary inferences are reasonable
based on the combined and cumulative force of all the evidence when viewed in the light most



         2
           See TEX. R. APP. P. 22.4 (providing that trial court must rule on motion in arrest of judgment within seventy-
five days after imposing or suspending sentence in open court, and motion is deemed denied if trial court has not ruled
on it when said period expires).


                                                          17
favorable to the verdict. Clayton, 235 S.W.3d at 778. “Circumstantial evidence is as probative as
direct evidence in establishing guilt, and circumstantial evidence alone can be sufficient to
establish guilt.” Rodriguez v. State, 521 S.W.3d 822, 827 (Tex. App.—Houston [1st Dist.] 2017,
no pet.) (citing Sorrells v. State, 343 S.W.3d 152, 155 (Tex. Crim. App. 2001)). Each fact need
not point directly and independently to the defendant’s guilt, provided that the cumulative force of
all the incriminating circumstances is sufficient to support the conviction. Hooper, 214 S.W.3d at
13. The factfinder may draw multiple reasonable inferences as long as each inference is supported
by the evidence presented at trial. Id. at 15.
         “A conviction cannot be had upon the testimony of an accomplice unless corroborated by
other evidence tending to connect the defendant with the offense committed[,] and the
corroboration is not sufficient if it merely shows the commission of the offense.” TEX. CODE CRIM.
PROC. ANN. art. 38.14. When evaluating the sufficiency of corroborating evidence under Article
38.14, we eliminate the accomplice testimony from consideration and then examine the remaining
portions of the record to see if there is any evidence that tends to connect the accused with the
commission of the crime. Castillo v. State, 221 S.W.3d 689, 691 (Tex. Crim. App. 2007). This
“tends to connect” standard does not present a high threshold, as the corroborating evidence need
not prove the defendant’s guilt beyond a reasonable doubt by itself. Solomon v. State, 49 S.W.3d
356, 361 (Tex. Crim. App. 2001); Turner v. State, 571 S.W.3d 283, 288 (Tex. App.—Texarkana
2019, pet. ref’d). “Rather, the [non-accomplice] evidence must simply link the accused in some
way to the commission of the crime[.]” Malone v. State, 253 S.W.3d 253, 257 (Tex. Crim. App.
2008).
         In determining whether the non-accomplice evidence tends to connect the defendant to the
commission of the offense, we view the non-accomplice evidence in the light most favorable to
the jury’s verdict. Turner, 571 S.W.3d at 288. We judge the sufficiency of the non-accomplice
evidence according to the particular facts and circumstances of each case. Smith v. State, 332
S.W.3d 425, 442 (Tex. Crim. App. 2011). When there are two permissible views of the evidence,
one tending to connect the defendant to the offense and the other not tending to connect the
defendant to the offense, appellate courts should defer to the view of the evidence chosen by the
factfinder.   Simmons v. State, 282 S.W.3d 504, 508 (Tex. Crim. App. 2009).               Direct or
circumstantial non-accomplice evidence constitutes sufficient corroboration if it shows that a




                                                 18
rational factfinder could have found that it sufficiently tended to connect the accused to the offense.
Smith, 332 S.W.3d at 442.

       No precise rule can be formulated as to the amount of evidence required to corroborate. The non-
       accomplice evidence does not need to be in itself sufficient to establish guilt beyond a reasonable
       doubt. Nor must the non-accomplice evidence directly link the accused to the commission of the
       offense. . . . Even apparently insignificant incriminating circumstances may sometimes afford
       satisfactory evidence of corroboration.


Dowthitt v. State, 931 S.W.2d 244, 249 (Tex. Crim. App. 1996) (citations omitted). Any
independent evidence tending to verify an accomplice witness’s version of events is corroborative,
even if it pertains only to a mere detail rather than a substantive connection between the defendant
and the offense. Lee v. State, 29 S.W.3d 570, 577 (Tex. App.—Dallas 2000, no pet.).
       “The corroboration requirement in Article 38.14 does not apply separately to each element
of the offense charged or to each aspect of the accomplice’s testimony. If that were so, there would
be no value in the testimony at all.” State v. Ambrose, 487 S.W.3d 587, 598 (Tex. Crim. App.
2016). “It is clearly unnecessary that the accomplice should be confirmed in every circumstance
which he details in evidence; for there would be no occasion to use him at all as a witness if his
narrative could be completely proved by other evidence free from all suspicion.” Holladay v.
State, 709 S.W.2d 194, 199 (Tex. Crim. App. 1986). “[R]ather, there need be only some non-
accomplice evidence tending to connect the defendant to the crime, not to every element of the
crime.” Joubert v. State, 235 S.W.3d 729, 731 (Tex. Crim. App. 2007).
Analysis
       A person commits the offense of directing the activities of a criminal street gang if, as part
of the gang’s identifiable leadership, he “knowingly finances, directs, or supervises the
commission of, or a conspiracy to commit, one or more of” the following, in pertinent part: (1) a
felony offense listed in Article 42A.054(a) of the Texas Code of Criminal Procedure or (2) a felony
offense in which a deadly weapon was used or exhibited during the commission of the offense by
members of a criminal street gang. TEX. PENAL CODE ANN. § 71.023(a)(1), (2). Murder is among
the offenses listed in Article 42A.054(a). TEX. CODE CRIM. PROC. ANN. art. 42A.054(a)(2).
Although Article 42A.054(a) does not identify aggravated assault as a predicate offense,
aggravated assault with a deadly weapon falls under Section 71.023(a)(2). TEX. PENAL CODE ANN.
§ 71.023(a)(2); Baker v. State, No. 02-17-00193-CR, 2020 WL 1808292, at *11 (Tex. App.—Fort
Worth Apr. 9, 2020, no pet.) (mem. op., not designated for publication) (holding that aggravated


                                                      19
assault with a deadly weapon falls under Subsection (a)(2) of Section 71.023). Therefore, both
murder and aggravated assault can serve as predicate offenses to support a conviction for directing
activities of a criminal street gang. TEX. PENAL CODE ANN. § 71.023(a)(1), (2); Baker, 2020 WL
1808292, at *11 (holding that “both murder and aggravated assault can be used as predicate
offenses to support a conviction for directing the activities of a criminal street gang under Penal
Code Section 71.023”).
       Appellant argues that, absent the testimony of Crowell, Valenzuela, Tibbits, and Griffin,
the evidence is legally insufficient to support his conviction. It is undisputed that Crowell,
Valenzuela, Tibbits, and Griffin are accomplices. Therefore, in analyzing the sufficiency of the
evidence, we must exclude their testimony from consideration and examine the remaining portions
of the record to determine whether there is any evidence that tends to connect Appellant to the
commission of the charged offense. See TEX. CODE CRIM. PROC. ANN. art. 38.14; Castillo, 221
S.W.3d at 691. The non-accomplice evidence included the testimony of Pearson, who testified
regarding the culture, protocols, and chain of command found in outlaw motorcycle gangs.
Pearson explained the split between UMC and the 1% Cossacks and the acts of violence that
ensued. Pearson explained that the national president of a gang exercises complete control and
approves everything that goes on in the gang, and the president’s total power shifts to the vice

as 2015, when the national president was unavailable and his power transferred to the vice
president (i.e. Appellant). Pearson recounted that in 2015, Appellant brokered the deal between
UMC and the Bandidos, and Appellant later became national president of UMC. In addition, the
non-accomplice evidence included the testimony of Railsback, who testified that the murder
investigation revealed that (1) Appellant became national president of UMC in March 2020, (2) in
the months before the murder, UMC members received a green light and were ordered to “smash
on sight” any members of the 1% Cossacks, (3) Appellant directly issued a smash-on-sight order

pushed the gang to become more violent, (5) Appellant instructed that every sergeant at arms

Appellant was at the “top of the chain” in UMC when Edwards’s murder occurred and (2) all

each aspect of the accomplices’ testimony. See Ambrose, 487 S.W.3d at 598; Joubert, 235 S.W.3d
at 731; Holladay, 709 S.W.2d at 199. When there are two permissible views of the evidence, one
of which tends to connect the defendant to the offense and the other which does not tend to connect
the defendant to the offense, we defer to the view of the evidence chosen by the jury. See
Simmons, 282 S.W.3d at 508. We conclude that the jury could rationally conclude that the
aforementioned non-accomplice evidence tends to connect Appellant to the offense, and that the
testimony of Crowell, Valenzuela, Tibbits, and Griffin was therefore sufficiently corroborated.
See Ambrose, 487 S.W.3d at 598; Castillo, 221 S.W.3d at 691; Solomon, 49 S.W.3d at 361; see

the activities of a criminal street gang. See Jackson, 443 U.S. at 319, 99 S. Ct. at 2786-87, 2789;
Brooks, 323 S.W.3d at 902 n.19; Clayton, 235 S.W.3d at 778; Hooper, 214 S.W.3d at 13;
Penagraph, 623 S.W.2d at 343; see also TEX. PENAL CODE ANN. § 71.023. Accordingly, we
overrule issue one.

         In issue two, Appellant argues that trial counsel provided ineffective assistance during

Standard of Review and Applicable Law
         To prevail on a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel, an appellant must satisfy a two-
pronged test:

         First, the defendant must show that counsel’s performance was deficient. This requires showing that
         counsel made errors so serious that counsel was not functioning as the “counsel” guaranteed the
         defendant by the Sixth Amendment. Second, the defendant must show that the deficient
         performance prejudiced the defense. This requires showing that counsel’s errors were so serious as
         to deprive the defendant of a fair trial, a trial whose result is reliable.


Strickland v. Washington, 466 U.S. 668, 687, 104 S. Ct. 2052, 2064, 80 L. Ed. 2d 674 (1984);
Hernandez v. State, 726 S.W.2d 53, 56-57 (Tex. Crim. App. 1986) (adopting Strickland test); see
also Tong v. State, 25 S.W.3d 707, 712 (Tex. Crim. App. 2000). To satisfy the first prong of the
Strickland test, an appellant must demonstrate that counsel’s representation fell below an objective
standard of reasonableness under prevailing professional norms. See Strickland, 466 U.S. at 688,
104 S. Ct. at 2064-65; Tong, 25 S.W.3d at 712.
         Under the second Strickland prong, an appellant must show that counsel’s deficient
performance prejudiced the defense. Strickland, 466 U.S. at 687, 104 S. Ct. at 2064; Tong, 25
S.W.3d at 712. It is not enough for an appellant to show that counsel’s alleged errors “had some
conceivable effect on the outcome of the proceedings.” Burruss v. State, 20 S.W.3d 179, 186
(Tex. App.—Texarkana 2000, pet. ref’d). Rather, an appellant must demonstrate a reasonable
probability that but for his counsel’s unprofessional errors, the outcome of his trial would have

investigate or present witnesses constitutes ineffective assistance only when “it is affirmatively

Analysis
       Appellant asserts that trial counsel provided ineffective assistance during guilt-innocence
and punishment by failing to (1) advise Appellant to surrender to authorities, (2) “substantially
review” discovery with Appellant, (3) retain an investigator, (4) develop a record of his concerns
that witnesses decided not to testify due to fear, and (5) object to hearsay evidence. In addition,
Appellant argues that trial counsel provided ineffective assistance by (1) waiting until after
deliberations to address the jury’s note expressing concerns about security, (2) failing to request
an evidentiary hearing on his motion for new trial, (3) presenting an inaccurate AI-prepared

       As discussed above, to establish ineffective assistance, an appellant must demonstrate that
(1) counsel’s performance was deficient and (2) counsel’s performance prejudiced his defense.
Strickland, 466 U.S. at 687, 104 S. Ct. at 2064; Hernandez, 726 S.W.2d at 56-57; see also Tong,
25 S.W.3d at 712. The first prong of the test requires a showing that counsel’s representation fell
below an objective standard of reasonableness under prevailing professional norms.              See
Strickland, 466 U.S. at 688, 104 S. Ct. at 2064-65. To establish prejudice as required by the
second prong of the test, an appellant must establish that, but for counsel’s alleged ineffective
assistance, the result of his trial would have been different. Id., 466 U.S. at 694, 104 S. Ct. at
2068; Bone, 77 S.W.3d at 833. In other words, it is not enough to show that counsel’s errors had
some conceivable effect on the outcome of the proceeding. Burruss, 20 S.W.3d at 186. Rather,
an appellant must show that, but for his attorney’s errors and omissions, the jury would have had
a reasonable doubt about his guilt or imposed a lesser punishment. See id.; see also Bone, 77
S.W.3d at 837. In determining the existence of prejudice, we consider the nature of the charged
offense and the strength of the evidence presented. Junell v. State, 607 S.W.3d 410, 417 (Tex.
App.—Texarkana 2020, pet. ref’d).
       Appellant was charged with the first-degree felony offense of directing the activities of a
criminal street gang whose members committed the predicate offenses of murder and aggravated
assault, and he faced a punishment range of imprisonment for twenty-five years to life. See TEX.
PENAL CODE ANN. §§ 12.32(a), 71.023(a), (b). As recounted in detail above, the State presented
strong evidence of Appellant’s guilt. See Junell, 607 S.W.3d at 417. Assuming without deciding
that counsel’s performance was deficient, we conclude that Appellant fails to establish that, but
for counsel’s alleged errors and omissions, the outcome of his trial would have been different. See
Strickland, 466 U.S. at 687, 697, 104 S. Ct. at 2064, 2069; Bone, 77 S.W.3d at 833. Appellant
failed to develop a record of any evidence that could have been presented to make a “not guilty”
verdict likely. Similarly, Appellant failed to develop a record of any testimony that likely would
mitigate his punishment. See Thompson, 9 S.W.3d at 813. Furthermore, most of the alleged errors
of which Appellant complains either had no effect or had only a very tangential effect on the actual
trial of his case. In other words, Appellant fails to demonstrate that, but for counsel’s alleged



                                                24
errors and omissions, the jury would have had a reasonable doubt about his guilt or imposed a
lesser punishment. See Burruss, 20 S.W.3d at 186; see also Bone, 77 S.W.3d at 837. Accordingly,
we overrule issue two.


                                                  DISPOSITION
         Having overruled issues one and two, we affirm the trial court’s judgment.
