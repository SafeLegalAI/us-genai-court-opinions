I. BACKGROUND
Petitioner, a citizen of Mexico, is detained at T. Don Hutto Detention Center in Taylor,
Texas. (Pet., Dkt. 1, at 4). He pleads that that he “has resided in the United States for more than
twenty years and is the beneficiary of a qualifying family-based visa petition that renders him eligible
for adjustment of status under INA § 245(i).” (Id.). More specifically, “Petitioner is the beneficiary of
a Form I-130, Petition for Alien Relative, filed by his United States citizen sister, with a priority date
of April 29, 2001 and case number SRC0121154017.” (Id. at 5). He claims that his “filing date
squarely qualifies him for adjustment of status under INA § 245(i), 8 U.S.C. § 1255(i), upon visa
availability.”3 (Id.). Petitioner also states that he “has no disqualifying criminal history, poses no
danger to the community, and has strong family and community ties in the United States.” (Id. at
11).
On March 17, 2026—approximately two weeks prior to the filing of the instant habeas
action—an immigration judge denied Petitioner bond, stating:
The Court finds that the Respondent poses a significant flight risk. Aside from
remaining in the United States for approximately 28 years after violating the terms of
his nonimmigrant visa, the Court observes that the Respondent worked without
employment authorization for several years during which time he failed to file federal
income tax returns and also has operated motor vehicles in the U.S. for several years
without a valid driver’s license. The Court also notes that the Respondent failed to
demonstrate property ties to the U.S. or his immediate eligibility to adjust status.
While the Respondent stated that he provides assistance to a 12-year-old girl in the
U.S. who he considers his daughter although he has not formally adopted her, the
Court finds the numerous negative equities present in the Respondent’s claim militate
against his release on bond because they indicate that he is less likely to appear for a
removal hearing.

II. ANALYSIS
It is well-settled that “‘[f]ederal courts are courts of limited jurisdiction,’ possessing ‘only that
power authorized by Constitution and statute.’” Gunn v. Minton, 568 U.S. 251, 256 (2013) (quoting
Kokkonen v. Guardian Life Ins. Co. of Am., 511 U.S. 375, 377 (1994)). Certain provisions of the
Immigration and Nationality Act (“INA”) divest federal court jurisdiction over certain claims.
Relevant to this action, Section § 1226(e) provides: “The Attorney General’s discretionary judgment
regarding the application of this section shall not be subject to review. No court may set aside any
action or decision by the Attorney General under this section regarding the detention of any alien or
the revocation or denial of bond or parole.” 8 U.S.C. § 1226(e). Respondents assert that, under this
subsection, the Court lacks jurisdiction to review a discretionary decision made by the Attorney
General through an immigration judge. (Resp., Dkt. 4, at 4).
The Fifth Circuit has held, however, that “[s]ection 1226(e) may strip us of jurisdiction to
review judgments designated as discretionary under the pertinent language of the statute, but it does
not deprive us of all authority to review statutory and constitutional challenges. We retain
jurisdiction to review [the petitioner’s] detention insofar as that detention presents constitutional
issues, such as those raised in a habeas petition.” Oyelude v. Chertoff, 125 F. App’x 543, 546 (5th Cir.
2005) (per curiam). See also Nielsen v. Preap, 586 U.S. 392, 401 (2019) (quoting Jennings v. Rodriguez, 583
U.S. 281, 295–96 (2018)) (stating that the jurisdictional bar in § 1226(e) “applies only to
‘discretionary’ decisions about the ‘application’ of § 1226 to particular cases. It does not block
lawsuits over ‘the extent of the Government's detention authority under the “statutory framework”
as a whole.’”); Zadvydas v. Davis, 533 U.S. 678, 688 (2001) (explaining that “the extent of the Attorney
General’s authority . . . is not a matter of discretion” and is therefore within a court’s jurisdiction to
review, even where the INA bars review “of the Attorney General’s exercise of discretion”); Gul v.
Rozos, 163 F. App’x 317, 319 (5th Cir. 2006) (per curiam) (“To the extent that [the petitioner’s]

Here, Petitioner has pleaded that, as of March 2026,5 Petitioner was “now eligible to apply
for adjustment of status and obtain lawful permanent residence.” (Pet., Dkt. 1, at 6). At his bond
hearing before an immigration judge, however, the immigration judge found Petitioner to be a
“significant flight risk,” in part because he “failed to demonstrate . . . his immediate eligibility to
adjust status.” (Immigr. J. Order, Dkt. 4-3, at 1). Given that Petitioner alleges that he was eligible or
imminently eligible to apply for adjustment of status and become a lawful permanent resident, it
appears possible that the immigration judge relied on inaccurate information in choosing to deny
Petitioner bond.
Some courts have held that, when an immigration judge denies bond based on false
information, the denial violates the petitioner’s right to due process. See, e.g., Montero-Martinez v.
Mattos, No. 2:25-CV-02391-CDS-DJA, 2026 WL 674497, at *1–2 (D. Nev. Mar. 9, 2026); Arellano v.
Sessions, No. 6:18-CV-06625-MAT, 2019 WL 3387210, at *7 (W.D.N.Y. July 26, 2019) (stating that
courts can consider whether an immigration judge “may have misinterpreted or failed to consider
probative evidence . . . on habeas review”). See also Roberts v. United States, 445 U.S. 552, 556 (1980)
(explaining that, in the criminal sentencing context, the Court has “sustained due process objections
to sentences imposed on the basis of ‘misinformation of constitutional magnitude’”). The Court will
