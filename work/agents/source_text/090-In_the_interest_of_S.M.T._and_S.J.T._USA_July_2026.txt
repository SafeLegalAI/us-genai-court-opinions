           Validity of the “Agreed Order” and Appellant’s Consent

      We first cumulatively address appellant’s complaints raising the ultimate
issue whether an “Agreed Order” was rendered on October, 14, 2024.

       Father’s Issues 1, 2, and 3, all go to whether the order was truly “agreed”
and properly entered as arguing they are framed as an unfair/defective entry
process (Issue 1), lack of actual consent/mutual assent to the agreed order (Issue 2),
and a concrete example of a material term Father says he did not agree to (standard
possession order language) (Issue 3).

      Trial courts have wide discretion in determining issues of custody, control,
possession, support, and visitation matters involving children. Gillespie v.
Gillespie, 644 S.W.2d 449, 451 (Tex. 1982). We review a trial court’s decision to
modify conservatorship for an abuse of discretion. Id. at 451.

      A judge’s oral pronouncement in open court constitutes a rendition. Texas
Family Code § 101.026. Rendition occurs through oral pronouncement when the
court reflects an intent to render a full, final, and complete judgment at that time.
Interest of C.E.H., No. 09-19-00120-CV, 2020 WL 5666081, at *2 (Tex. App.—
Beaumont Sept. 24, 2020, no pet.) citing See Araujo v. Araujo, 493 S.W.3d 232,
235–36 (Tex. App.—San Antonio 2016, no pet.)(illustrating acceptable statements
such as “your divorce is granted” or “I’ll grant your divorce today”) (emphasis
added). Such an oral rendition is distinct from subsequent reduction to writing,

                                          8
signing, and entry. State v. Naylor, 466 S.W.3d 783, 788 (Tex. 2015) (quoting S &
A Rest. Corp. v. Leal, 892 S.W.2d 855, 857 (Tex. 1995)).

      Pursuant to Texas Rule of Civil Procedure 11, settlement agreements must
be in writing, signed, and filed with the court, or must be made in open court and
entered of record. See Tex. R. Civ. P. 11. A Rule 11 agreement must be interpreted
by the trial court based on the intention of the parties as expressed in the entire
agreement in light of the surrounding circumstances, including the state of the
pleadings, the allegations therein and the attitude of the parties with respect to the
issues. In re Acevedo, 956 S.W.2d 770, 775 (Tex. App.—San Antonio 1997, no
writ.). A party may revoke his consent to settle a case anytime before judgment is
rendered. Samples Exterminators v. Samples, 640 S.W.2d 873, 874–75 (Tex. 1982)
citing Burnaman v. Heaton, 150 Tex. 333, 339, 240 S.W.2d 288, 291 (1951).

      While approval of a settlement, standing alone, does not necessarily
constitute rendition, the dispositive question is whether the court officially
announced its decision; when it does, rendition occurs then rather than at the
subsequent signing. S & A Rest. Corp. v. Leal, 892 S.W.2d 855, 857 (Tex. 1995).
After both parties acknowledged the terms of the agreement and that its effect
would be immediate, the court announced that it had accepted the terms of the
agreement stated on the record and that the agreement was effective today.
Though Mother and Father did not each address every single term, Father
confirmed and agreed to the terms identified by Mother at the hearing.         Despite
reluctance noted by appellant, when a trial court, as here, accepts the agreement on
the   record   and   signals   immediate      disposition,   rendition   is   complete
notwithstanding the ministerial later acts of drafting and signing. See Wharton v.
Gonzales, 761 S.W.2d 72, 74 (Tex. App.—Houston [14th Dist.] 1988, no
writ)(affirming judgment based on agreed order where appellant indicated she had

                                          9
felt pressured to enter the agreement).             Therefore, Father’s principal complaint
fails because the trial court rendered judgment on the record at the October 14
hearing when it accepted the parties’ agreement by the terms stated on the record,
and made the orders contemporaneously effective despite later reduction to writing
and signature. See id.

       Father’s issues concerning the propriety of the Agreed Order based on the
parties’ orally announced Rule 11 agreement are overruled.

Challenge to the ambiguity of the agreed order, regarding primary residence

         We last consider Father’s complaints to the ambiguity of the court’s order,
specifically his complaint of alleged ambiguity and resulting error concerning the
children’s “primary residence.” In conjunction with this complaint he argues that it
should read exclusively that his address within the Clear Creek ISD is the primary
residence.

         Presuming the issue is not waived or rendered moot, we address Father’s
complaint to the ambiguity of the agreement, specifically the primary residence.
First, importantly we note that Father first pleaded in his Petition to Modify Parent-
Child Relationship that “[t]he children’s primary residence should be in the


                                          11
attendance zone of Stewart Elementary School and not just one specific address.”4
While this language disappeared in subsequent petitions, the parties agreed on the
record that the children’s primary residence shall be within Clear Creek ISD, and
the children shall attend the schools in that district regardless of where either
parent lives.

       In the Agreed Order in Suit to Modify Parent–Child Relationship (final
modification order), the order does not set a specific street address. Instead, it
imposes a school-district geographic restriction through the following provisions:

                        [No primary conservator designation]

       “IT IS ORDERED that neither parent shall be designated as the
       primary conservator of the children.”
                [Geographic restriction / primary residence language]

       “IT IS ORDERED that the primary residence of the children shall be
       within Clear Creek Independent School District, and the parties shall
       not remove the children from Clear Creek Independent School
       District, for the purpose of changing the primary residence of the
       children until this geographic restriction is modified by further order
       of the court of continuing jurisdiction or by a written agreement that is
       signed by the parties and filed with that court.”
                            [School enrollment provision]

       “IT IS ORDERED that the children shall attend the schools in Clear
       Creek Independent School District regardless of where [Mother] or
       [Father] live.”
       These provision read together implicitly require that one or the other parent
must live within the school district. Even when read in isolation, the school
enrollment provision carries this same meaning and is not ambiguous. See Foard
Cnty. v. Sandifer, 105 Tex. 420, 424, 151 S.W. 523, 524 (1912)(“It is a well-

established rule of construction that language in a contract which is susceptible of
two constructions, one of which would render the contract illegal, and the other
would make it lawful, that contract which would conform the contract to the law
must be adopted.”); see also Borgelt v. Austin Firefighters Ass’n, IAFF Local 975,
692 S.W.3d 288, 303 n.19 (Tex. 2024). The modification allows both parents the
ability to move as long as either remains within the school district. The parties did
not agree on the record or otherwise that appellant’s address exclusively would be
the primary residence.

      Under the Family Code, a trial court need not appoint a parent with the right
to determine a child’s primary residence if the parents have adopted an agreed
parenting plan that, among other things, provides that ‘the child’s primary
residence shall be within a specified geographical area.’          Tex. Fam. Code
153.133(c); See also Interest of A.I.M., No. 01-22-00409-CV, 2023 WL 8192530,
at *8 (Tex. App.—Houston [1st Dist.] Nov. 28, 2023, no pet.). The primary
residence terms in the final modification order are consistent with the requirements
of the Family Code, and is consistent with the terms of the agreement announced
and agreed to at the October 14 hearing.

      Accordingly, we overrule appellant’s complaints that the order is ambiguous
or contrary to what is legally permissible with respect to the children’s primary
address or that it should read exclusively that his address within the Clear Creek
ISD is the primary residence.

                                 III. APPELLANT’S BRIEF

      Finally, some discussion of appellant’s brief is warranted. As previously
mentioned, appellant’s brief is disjointed and difficult to follow. However, that is
not the only shortcoming of appellant’s briefing. It is obvious that appellant’s brief
was either (1) the result of using artificial intelligence yielding hallucination
                                           13
citations or (2) a deliberate attempt to mislead this court. I prefer to assume the use
of AI rather than intentional deception.

      Appellant’s attorney, Jerry Lytel Lavespere, III, signed appellant’s brief.
Unfortunately, the brief is far from accurate. On page 8 of Mr. Lavespere’s brief,
he states: “As held in In re Marriage of Harrison, 557 S.W.3d 99, 111 (Tex.
App.—Houston [14th Dist.] 2018, pet. denied), a trial court’s failure to consider
valid objections to a proposed order can constitute reversible error. The court
stated that ‘fundamental notions of due process require that parties be afforded a
reasonable opportunity to be heard on issues affecting their rights.’” Although the
Marriage of Harrison case exists, the passage that Mr. Lavespere quoted is entirely
fabricated.

      Unfortunately, that is not the only instance of fabricated or hallucinated
quotations. On page 11, appellant writes “As noted in In re Marriage of Swim, 291
S.W.3d 500, 505 (Tex. App.—Amarillo 2009, no pet.), ‘vague, indefinite terms in
divorce Orders are susceptible to multiple interpretations, which inevitably lead to
further litigation.’”   Again, although that case exists, the quoted language is
fabricated. On page 12 of appellant’s brief, Mr. Lavespere states: “Geographic
restrictions in custody orders are directly tied to these principles, as established in
Lenz v. Lenz, 79 S.W.3d 10, 14 (Tex. 2002), where the Texas Supreme Court held
that such restrictions serve the dual purposes of (1) ensuring children’s continued
contact with both parents and (2) minimizing disruption to children’s education
and community ties.” These dual purposes appear nowhere in Lenz. See id.

      Other parts of appellant’s brief are equally puzzling. For example, on page 1
of the brief, appellant cites “Dickerson v. State, 685 S.W.2d 132” for the
proposition that he requests oral argument, even though Dickerson does nothing to
support that proposition.     See id. at 133 (in a case concerning the State’s

                                           14
representation and participation in a criminal prosecution and appeal, the court
mentioned oral argument to note that under then-applicable rules that oral
argument could only be requested after the filing of the brief). Similarly, on page
11, appellant states that “In In re A.S., 298 S.W.3d 834, 836 (Tex.App.—Amarillo
2009, no pet.), the court held that orders regarding conservatorship must be
specific enough to be enforceable by contempt.” The A.S. case has nothing to do
with contempt. See id.

      The problem with fake citations and quotations has become rampant. While
courts might ignore hallucination cases and quotations from pro se litigants, we
cannot turn a blind eye when lawyers commit the same transgressions.              Our
Supreme Court is not unmindful of the problem.             The court has proposed
amendments to Texas Rules of Appellate Procedure 9.1 that mandate that by
signing a court filing, the signer represents to the court that the “signer reviewed
the filing and verified its accuracy, including that the legal citations, quotations,
and holdings are not fabricated.” This proposed rule then outlines sanctions that
can be imposed for a violation of this representation. Unfortunately, this is a
proposed rule that has just recently been published and is expected to take effect on
Oct. 1, 2026. The proposed rule also notes that this does not change an appellate
court’s existing authority to impose sanctions. Even before the proposed rule,
Texas courts of appeals have always had inherent authority to impose sanctions for
improper conduct. In re Terminix Intern., Co., L.P., 131 S.W.3d 651, 654 (Tex.
App.—Corpus Christi–Edinburg 2004, no pet.); see also In re Bennett, 960 S.W.2d
35, 40 (Tex. 1997). Although appellee did not request sanctions, on our own
motion we order attorney Lavespere to attend three hours of Continuing Legal
Education on the dangers of the use of artificial intelligence in court filings and to
certify to this court such attendance and compliance within sixty days of this
