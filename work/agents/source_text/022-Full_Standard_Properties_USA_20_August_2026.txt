LEGAL STANDARD
A party may file a motion for “attorney’s fees and related nontaxable expenses” within

25

fourteen days after entry of judgment. Fed. R. Civ. P. 54(d)(2). “Rule 54(d)(2) creates a

26

procedure but not a right to recover attorneys’ fees.” MRO Commc’ns, Inc. v. Am. Tel. & Tel. Co.,

27

197 F.3d 1276, 1280 (9th Cir. 1999). “[T]here must be another source of authority for such an

28

award.” Id. at 1281.
3

2

calculating the fees.” Close v. Sotheby’s, Inc., 909 F.3d 1204, 1208 (9th Cir. 2018) (internal

3

quotation marks and citation omitted). “California permits parties to allocate attorney’s fees by

4

contract.” Berkla v. Corel Corp., 302 F.3d 909, 919 (9th Cir. 2002) (citing Cal. Civ. Proc. Code §

5

1021). “California also allows attorney’s fees authorized by contract, statute, or law to be

6

recovered as costs” by the prevailing party. Id. (citing Cal. Civ. Proc. Code § 1033.5(a)(10)).

7

If a claim for attorneys’ fees is grounded in a contractual fee-shifting provision, entitlement

8

to fees is circumscribed by California Civil Code § 1717. See Berkla, 302 F.3d at 919. Section

9

1717 provides in relevant part that:

In any action on a contract, where the contract specifically provides that attorney’s
fees and costs, which are incurred to enforce that contract, shall be awarded either
to one of the parties or to the prevailing party, then the party who is determined to
be the party prevailing on the contract, whether he or she is the party specified in
the contract or not, shall be entitled to reasonable attorney’s fees in addition to
other costs.

14

Cal. Civ. Code § 1717(a) (emphasis added). “Where an action has been voluntarily dismissed or

15

dismissed pursuant to a settlement of the case, there shall be no prevailing party for purposes of

16

this section.” Cal. Civ. Proc. Code § 1717(b)(2).

17

Thus, attorneys’ fees incurred in defending claims brought “on a contract” cannot be

18

recovered under a fee-shifting provision contained in that contract if the action was voluntarily

19

dismissed. See Santisas v. Goodin, 17 Cal. 4th 599, 602 (1998) (“We conclude that in voluntary

20

pretrial dismissal cases, Civil Code section 1717 bars recovery of attorney fees incurred in

21

defending contract claims[.]”). Attorneys’ fees incurred in defending tort or other noncontract

22

claims may be recovered under a contractual fee-shifting provision after voluntary dismissal of the

23

action, however, as those fees are not barred by California Civil Code § 1717. See id. “Whether

24

attorney fees incurred in defending tort or other noncontract claims are recoverable after a pretrial

25

dismissal depends upon the terms of the contractual attorney fee provision.” Id.

26

If a party is entitled to attorneys’ fees, California law provides for calculation of those fees

27

using the lodestar method. See Ketchum v. Moses, 24 Cal. 4th 1122, 1131-32 (2001) (citing

28

Serrano v. Priest, 20 Cal. 3d 25, 48 (1977)).

III.

DISCUSSION

2

Defendant brings this motion under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 54(d), seeking an

3

award of attorneys’ fees in the amount of $93,226 and costs in the amount of $792.38. Defendant

4

acknowledges that any attorneys’ fees award would be subject to a setoff for the $14,500 in

5

attorneys’ fees already paid to Defendant by Plaintiff’s counsel. Plaintiff disputes Defendant’s

6

entitlement to attorneys’ fees and, in the alternative, asks that the Court substantially reduce the

7

fees requested by Defendant. Plaintiff does not address Defendant’s request for costs.

8

The Court briefly addresses Defendant’s request for $792.38 in costs before turning to the

9

more substantive issues relating to Defendant’s request for attorneys’ fees. As noted above, Rule

10

54(d) allows a party to file a post-judgment motion for “attorney’s fees and related nontaxable

11

expenses.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 54(d)(2). Defendant seeks “$792.38 associated with filing fees and

12

related litigation expenses.” Kao Decl. ¶ 17, ECF 33-1. Court filing fees are taxable costs and

13

therefore do not constitute nontaxable expenses that may be recovered in an attorneys’ fees

14

motion. See 28 U.S.C. § 1920 (listing taxable costs). It is unclear what the “related litigation

15

expenses” were. The Court finds that Defendant has not offered adequate support for recovery of

16

allowable nontaxable expenses.

17

Defendant’s motion is DENIED as to its request for costs in the amount of $792.38.

imposed by California Civil Code § 1717, discussed below, Defendant became the prevailing

2

party under California law when Plaintiff voluntarily dismissed this action without prejudice. See

3

Cal. Civ. Proc. Code § 1032(a)(4) (“‘Prevailing party’ includes . . . a defendant in whose favor a

4

dismissal is entered[.]”); Cano v. Glover, 143 Cal. App. 4th 326, 331 (2006) (a defendant in whose

5

6

§ 1032(a)(4) whether the dismissal is with or without prejudice). Accordingly, Defendant may

7

recover as costs any attorneys’ fees authorized under the loan agreement. See Berkla, 302 F.3d at

8

919 (“California also allows attorney’s fees authorized by contract, statute, or law to be recovered

9

as costs” by the prevailing party.).

10

Defendant’s prevailing party status and resulting right to recover attorneys’ fees under the

11

loan agreement are limited by California Civil Code § 1717. Under § 1717, there is no prevailing

12

party in an action “on a contract” if the action is dismissed voluntarily. See Cal. Civ. Code §

13

1717(b)(2). Thus, attorneys’ fees incurred in defending claims brought “on a contract” cannot be

14

recovered under a fee-shifting provision in that contract if the action was voluntarily dismissed.

15

See Santisas, 17 Cal. 4th at 602. Defendant argues that § 1717 does not apply here because none

16

of the claims asserted in this action are brought “on a contract.” Defendant correctly points out

17

that most of the claims asserted in the complaint are either statutory or tort claims, not contract

18

claims. Plaintiff nonetheless argues that all claims in the complaint are brought “on a contract”

19

because they relate to the parties’ obligations under the loan agreement.

20

“Although it has been noted that the exact reach of the term, ‘on a contract,’ can be

21

difficult to discern, one test which has been distilled from the case law is to ask whether (1) the

22

action (or cause of action) involves an agreement in the sense that the action (or cause of action)

23

arises out of, is based upon, or relates to an agreement by seeking to define or interpret its terms or

24

to determine or enforce a party’s rights or duties under the agreement, and (2) the agreement

25

contains an attorney fees clause.” Khan v. Shim, 7 Cal. App. 5th 49, 57 n.6 (2016) (internal

26

quotation marks, citation, and brackets omitted). “In other words, a defining characteristic of an

27

action ‘on a contract’ is that the cause of action must seek to define or interpret the contract’s

28

terms or to determine or enforce a party’s rights or duties under the agreement.” Id. (internal
6

United States District Court
Northern District of California

1

quotation marks, citation, and brackets omitted).

2

In Khan, the appellate court applied this test to a dispute between parties to a contract

3

involving the sale of a dental practice, which contained an attorney fee provision. See Khan, 7

4

Cal. App. 5th at 52. The plaintiff voluntarily dismissed her complaint containing both contract

5

and tort claims without prejudice, and the trial court awarded attorneys’ fees to the defendant for

6

his defense against the complaint as a whole. See id. On appeal, the Khan court found that the

7

award of attorneys’ fees for defense of the entire action was in error, because California Civil

8

Code § 1717 barred an award of fees for defense of the plaintiff’s contract claims. See id. The

9

parties agreed that the plaintiff’s claims for breach of contract and rescission were on the contract

10

for purposes of § 1717, and the Khan court found that position to be “in line with relevant

11

authority.” Id. at 57 n.6. The appellate court remanded the case with direction that the trial court

12

should determine which fees could be awarded for defense of the tort claims alleged in the

13

complaint, and whether the fees could be allocated between the tort claims as to which fees were

14

recoverable and the contract claims as to which fees were not recoverable. See id. at 52.

15

Consistent with the Khan court’s determination that the rescission claim in that case was

16

“on a contract,” this Court likewise finds that Plaintiff’s Fifth Cause of Action for rescission or

17

reformation of the loan agreement (hereinafter, “rescission claim”) was “on a contract” for

18

purposes of § 1717. However, the other substantive claims asserted in Plaintiff’s complaint were

19

not “on a contract,” as they were torts or statutory claims in the nature of torts. Plaintiff argues

20

that those claims were “on a contract” because they related to the loan agreement, which governs

21

the parties’ rights and obligations. Plaintiff also appears to argue that if the loan agreement’s fee

22

provision applies to the claims in this case, those claims should be considered “on a contract.”

23

The Court finds Plaintiff’s argument to be unpersuasive. The tort and statutory claims asserted in

24

the complaint were not “on a contract” for purposes of § 1717 merely because they related to the

25

loan agreement and fell within the broad fee provision contained in the loan agreement. See

26

Santisas, 17 Cal. 4th at 619 (§ 1717 did not apply to fraud claims relating to the contract); Khan, 7

27

Cal. App. 5th at 62 (fraud claims were not “on a contract” even though they arose out of and

28

related to provisions of the contract).

Calculation of Attorneys’ Fees

California law provides for calculation of attorneys’ fees using the lodestar method, which

11

“begins with a touchstone or lodestar, based on the ‘careful compilation of time spent and

12

reasonable hourly compensation of each attorney.’” Ketchum, 24 Cal. 4th at 1131-32 (quoting

13

Serrano v. Priest, 20 Cal. 3d 25, 48 (1977)); see also PLCM Grp. v. Drexler, 22 Cal. 4th 1084,

14

1095 (2000) (“[T]he fee setting inquiry in California ordinarily begins with the ‘lodestar.’”). “The

15

reasonable hourly rate is that prevailing in the community for similar work.” PLCM, 22 Cal. 4th

16

at 1095. “The lodestar figure may then be adjusted, based on consideration of factors specific to

17

the case,” including “the nature of the litigation, its difficulty, the amount involved, the skill

18

required in its handling, the skill employed, the attention given, the success or failure, and other

19

circumstances in the case.” Id. at 1095-96.

20

The hours defense counsel spent on this case are set forth in a chart submitted as Exhibit G

21

to the declaration of Defendant’s counsel, Ms. Kao. See Kao Decl. Ex. G, ECF 33-1. The chart

22

identifies the hours each attorney and legal secretary spent on each major task in the case and the

23

applicable hourly billing rate. See id. Ms. Kao has submitted a supplemental declaration

24

regarding additional hours spent by counsel in preparing the reply in support of the present motion

25

and attending the hearing. See Suppl. Kao Decl. ¶ 5, ECF 37-1.

26

The Court will not award fees for clerical work performed by legal secretaries. The Court

27

considers those tasks to be subsumed in normal overhead expenses. See Zuegel v. Mountain View

28

Police Dep’t, No. 17-CV-03249-BLF, 2021 WL 2808977, at *10 (N.D. Cal. July 6, 2021), aff’d,
8

1

No. 21-16277, 2024 WL 1007500 (9th Cir. Mar. 8, 2024) (collecting cases). However, the Court

2

is satisfied that all of the attorney time spent in defending this case is reasonable, particularly

3

given the necessity of litigating Plaintiff’s application for a temporary restraining order in the state

4

court and filing a motion to dismiss in the district court. The Court also finds that the hourly rates

5

charged by counsel, ranging between $375 and $750, are in line with rates charged in this

6

community for similar work. See t'Bear v. Forman, No. 17-CV-00796-JSC, 2022 WL 2343037, at

7

*4 (N.D. Cal. June 29, 2022) (collecting cases). Deducting the fees for eight hours of secretarial

8

work ($2,720) from the total fees requested ($93,226), the Court finds that Defendant reasonably

incurred $90,506 in attorneys’ fees to defend this case as a whole.
