                                           II

       Separate and apart from the fact that the Potterfs’ objections do not comply

 with Rule 11, they fail on the merits.

                                            A

       The Court begins with the Potterfs’ argument that the Magistrate Judge erred

 when she recommended that the Court deny their motion to enjoin Joshua’s state-

 court criminal prosecution. As the Sixth Circuit has explained, “federal injunctions

 against a state criminal law enforcement process [should] be issued only ‘under

 extraordinary circumstances where the danger of irreparable loss is both great and

 immediate.’” Fieger v. Cox, 524 F.3d 770, 774 (6th Cir. 2008) (quoting Younger v.



                                            5
Case 1:26-cv-10860-MFL-PTM ECF No. 107, PageID.1555 Filed 08/26/26 Page 6 of 16




 Harris, 401 U.S. 37, 45 (1971)). See also Ohio C.R. Comm’n v. Dayton Christian

 Schs., 477 U.S. 619, 626 (1986) (“[A] federal court should not enjoin a pending state

 criminal proceeding except in the very unusual situation that an injunction is

 necessary to prevent great and immediate irreparable injury”). The Potterfs have not

 satisfied that very demanding standard.

       The Potterfs insist that a stay of the state court criminal proceedings against

 Joshua is essential because (1) Joshua has mental health disabilities and financial

 limitations that prevent him from filing papers in-person or by regular mail and (2)

 the state court has refused to accommodate Joshua by permitting him to

 electronically file papers. (See Obj., ECF No. 80, PageID.944-946.) But the Potterfs

 have failed to persuade the Court that Joshua would suffer great and immediate harm

 without the ability to electronically file papers in his criminal case. As the docket

 sheet in Joshua’s criminal case shows, he has been represented by counsel

 throughout those proceedings. (See St. Ct. Dkt., ECF No. 104-3.) And he has told

 this Court repeatedly that he does not want to represent himself in that case. (See,

 e.g., ECF No. 24, PageID.136; ECF No. 78-2, PageID.925; ECF No. 78,

 PageID.915.) Since Joshua has a lawyer who has the ability to file papers in Joshua’s

 case, Joshua has no urgent need to file papers on his own behalf. Nor does Joshua

 have any general right to file papers in addition to those filed by his lawyer on his

 behalf. See, e.g., United States v, McNoriell, 176 F.4th 413, 425 (6th Cir. 2026)



                                           6
Case 1:26-cv-10860-MFL-PTM ECF No. 107, PageID.1556 Filed 08/26/26 Page 7 of 16




 (explaining that a criminal defendant has “no constitutional right to hybrid

 representation” in which he represents himself at the same time that he is represented

 by counsel) (internal quotation omitted); People v. Kevorkian, 639 N.W.2d 291, 320

 (Mich. App. 2001) (affirming denial of defendant’s request for hybrid

 representation). Simply put, Joshua has not shown that prohibiting him from filing

 papers electronically while he is represented by counsel has caused or will cause him

 to suffer great and immediate irreparable injury.       Thus, the Magistrate Judge

 correctly concluded that the Court should deny the request to enjoin Joshua’s state

 court proceedings.

       The Potterfs counter that the fact that Joshua has been represented by

 appointed counsel in his criminal case is essentially meaningless because, according

 to the Potterfs, Joshua has been “completely abandoned” by his attorneys. (Mot.,

 ECF No. 78, PageID.915.) The Potterfs are mistaken. Their belief that Joshua has

 been “abandoned” by his appointed attorneys appears to be premised largely on the

 fact that those attorneys would not take steps to remove his state criminal case to

 federal court. (See id.: “Court-appointed defense counsel Michael Andrew

 Murawski has completely abandoned his indigent client, maintaining a total

 communication blackout and completely ignoring a time-sensitive June 19, 2026,

 formal demand regarding mandatory federal removal actions under 28 U.S.C. §

 1455(b)(1).”) Indeed, Joshua sent one of his appointed attorneys an email stating



                                           7
Case 1:26-cv-10860-MFL-PTM ECF No. 107, PageID.1557 Filed 08/26/26 Page 8 of 16




 that the attorney’s failure to take action to remove Joshua’s case to federal court

 would be tantamount to a failure to provide “critical” legal services and that removal

 was a “formal, non-negotiable demand regarding your court-appointed

 representation of my defense.” (Joshua Email, ECF No. 63-4, PageID.598.)

       But any attempt by counsel to effectuate the removal demanded by Joshua

 would have been frivolous. The only removal statute that could possibly authorize

 the removal of Joshua’s state court criminal case is 28 U.S.C. § 1443(1).2 For a case

 to be properly removed under Section 1443(1), “the right denied [the] defendant

 must be one that arises under a federal law that provides for specific civil rights

 stated in terms of racial equality.” Conrad v. Robinson, 871 F.2d 612, 614-15 (6th

 Cir. 1989) (emphasis added). “Broad general allegations of deprivation of civil rights

 are not sufficient to permit removal” under Section 1443(1). State of Ohio v.

 Denman, 462 F.2d 1292, 1292 (6th Cir. 1972) (per curiam). Since Joshua does not

 allege that the state court prosecution infringes his right to racial equality, he could

 not remove that prosecution under Section 1443(1). Because the removal demanded

 by Joshua would have been frivolous, his attorneys did not abandon him when they

 declined to effectuate the removal. And because they did not abandon him, he has

 no pressing need to electronically file papers on his own behalf.

       Moreover, the Potterfs are mistaken in their apparent belief that the refusal by

 Joshua’s attorneys to follow his direction as to any and all matters related to his

 defense amounts to abandonment. The Potterfs ignore “the longstanding and sound

 principle that matters of trial strategy are left to counsel’s discretion.” Dixon v.

 Houk, 737 F.3d 1003, 1012 (6th Cir. 2013). Thus, while it is “recognized that the

 accused has the ultimate authority to make certain fundamental decisions regarding

 the case, [such] as to whether to plead guilty, waive a jury, testify in his or her own

 behalf, or take an appeal,” an indigent defendant does not have a “constitutional right

 to compel appointed counsel to press nonfrivolous points requested by the client, if

 counsel, as a matter of professional judgment, decides not to present those points.”

 Jones v. Barnes, 463 U.S. 745, 751 (1983).

       Next, the Potterfs appear to suggest that blocking Joshua from electronic filing

 that electronic filing is the only way for Joshua to present concerns about the

 performance of his lawyers to the state court. More specifically, the Potterfs have

 not shown why Joshua could not raise these concerns in a motion filed through

 regular mail. They say that they do not have a printer and have very limited financial

 resources, but they have not shown why Joshua could not handwrite a motion raising

       The only one of these recommendations that the Potterfs object to is the

 recommended denial of their cross-motion.          The Potterfs argue that that

 recommendation relied “on a total misinterpretation of local rules.” (Obj., ECF No.

 947.) They insist that no local rule prevents them from combining a motion and

 separate cross-motion in a single filing. They are mistaken. Rule 5 of the Court’s

 rules on electronic filing plainly prohibits the Potterfs’ cross-motion. That rule

 provides that “[m]otions must not be combined with any other stand-alone

 document. For example, [….] [a] counter-motion must not be combined with a

 response or reply.” (Eastern District of Michigan Electronic Filing Policies and

 Procedures Rev. 11/2025.) That is precisely what the Potterfs attempted to do here.

 The Court therefore OVERRULES this objection and ADOPTS the Magistrate

 Judge’s recommendation to deny the Potterfs’ cross-motion.

       With respect to the Magistrate Judge’s recommendations regarding the other

 pending motions, no party has filed any objections to those recommendations. The

 failure to object to an R&R releases the Court from its duty to independently review

 the matter. See Thomas v. Arn, 474 U.S. 140, 149 (1985). See also Ivey v. Wilson,

 832 F.2d 950, 957 (6th Cir. 1987) (explaining that where party fails to file “timely
