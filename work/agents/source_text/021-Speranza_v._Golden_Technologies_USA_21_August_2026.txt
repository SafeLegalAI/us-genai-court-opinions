Plaintiff Joseph Speranza, doing business as Premier Vegas Mobility Scooter Rental, sues

9 defendant Golden Technologies, Inc. asserting numerous claims arising out of the purchase of
10 mobility scooters. He used the scooters in the operation of a mobility scooter rental business that
11 he characterizes as a sole proprietorship. According to Speranza’s complaint, shortly after the
12 purchase, he began receiving customer complaints that the scooters stopped working despite a
13 full battery charge. He contends that he tried to work with Golden to resolve the issues but
14 Golden ultimately ceased communication and refused refunds for the allegedly defective
15 scooters. He asserts claims for strict products liability, negligence, breach of express and
16 implied warranties, intentional and negligent misrepresentation, unjust enrichment, and business
17 defamation. He also requests declaratory and injunctive relief, as well as compensatory and
18 punitive damages.
19

Golden moves to dismiss, arguing that Speranza lacks standing to assert the claims

20 because, based on his allegations, the real party in interest is a business entity and he cannot
21 represent an entity pro se. Golden argues that the business entity that suffered the alleged
22 injuries cannot be the purported sole proprietorship that Speranza identifies in the complaint
23 because he registered that fictitious business entity after the events at issue here.

Under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 36, matters addressed in requests for admissions

4 are admitted and “conclusively established” unless the responding party serves an answer or
5 objection within 30 days or unless otherwise stipulated or ordered by the court. Fed. R. Civ. P.
6 36(a)(3), (b). Golden served requests for admissions on Speranza to which he did not respond.
7 ECF Nos. 17-2; 17-11 at 3, 17; 17-12 at 3-4. Speranza did not respond to Golden’s summary
8 judgment motion, so he has not presented evidence that he responded to the requests for
9 admissions. Additionally, Speranza has not moved to withdraw or amend the admissions.
10 Accordingly, the requests for admissions are deemed admitted, and all admitted matters are now
11 conclusively established in this case.
12 II. Golden has not established as a matter of law that Speranza is not the real party in
13 interest, but given the admissions and lack of evidence to support Speranza’s claims,
14 Golden is entitled to summary judgment on all claims.
15

Summary judgment is appropriate if the movant shows “there is no genuine dispute as to

16 any material fact and the movant is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.” Fed. R. Civ. P.
17 56(a). A fact is material if it “might affect the outcome of the suit under the governing law.”
18 Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242, 248 (1986). A dispute is genuine if “the evidence
19 is such that a reasonable jury could return a verdict for the nonmoving party.” Id.
20

The party seeking summary judgment bears the initial burden of informing the court of

21 the basis for its motion and identifying those portions of the record that demonstrate the absence
22 of a genuine issue of material fact. Celotex Corp. v. Catrett, 477 U.S. 317, 323 (1986). The
23 burden then shifts to the non-moving party to set forth specific facts demonstrating there is a

1 genuine issue of material fact for trial. Sonner v. Schwabe N. Am., Inc., 911 F.3d 989, 992 (9th
2 Cir. 2018) (“To defeat summary judgment, the nonmoving party must produce evidence of a
3 genuine dispute of material fact that could satisfy its burden at trial.”). I view the evidence and
4 reasonable inferences in the light most favorable to the non-moving party. Zetwick v. Cnty. of
5 Yolo, 850 F.3d 436, 440-41 (9th Cir. 2017).

A. Golden has not established that no genuine dispute remains regarding who is the

7 real party in interest and whether Speranza can proceed pro se.
8

The “privilege to represent oneself pro se provided by [28 U.S.C.] § 1654 is personal to

9 the litigant and does not extend to other parties or entities.” Simon v. Hartford Life, Inc., 546
10 F.3d 661, 664 (9th Cir. 2008). “Consequently, in an action brought by a pro se litigant, the real
11 party in interest must be the person who by substantive law has the right to be enforced.” Id.
12 (quotation omitted). However, a litigant may proceed pro se to represent his sole proprietorship.
13 Sharemaster v. U.S. Sec. & Exch. Comm’n, 847 F.3d 1059, 1066 n.4 (9th Cir. 2017) (citing
14 Holdings AVV v. PDVSA Petroleo S.A., 506 F.3d 350, 354 n.4 (4th Cir. 2007) (“[A] sole
15 proprietorship has no legal existence apart from its owner, and . . . an individual owner may
16 represent his sole proprietorship in a pro se capacity.”)).
17

Golden argues that no genuine dispute remains that Speranza is not the real party in

18 interest because he admitted that the losses sustained were business losses and he cannot
19 represent a business entity pro se. For example, Speranza admitted that he purchased the
20 scooters in “a commercial rental business,” lost “business revenue,” and suffered reputation harm
21 “to a business.” ECF No. 17-3 at 5. He also admitted that in a prior state court case involving the
22 same scooters, the state court found that the real party in interest was a business entity, that
23 Speranza had identified Premier Vegas Mobility Scooter Rentals as an entity he operated, and

B. I grant Golden’s motion for summary judgment because Speranza has admitted

15 facts fatal to his claims or has not pointed to facts to support his claims.
16

1. Speranza has presented no evidence of a product defect.

17

To succeed on a strict products liability claim under Nevada law, “a plaintiff must show

18 that: 1) the product had a defect which rendered it unreasonably dangerous, 2) the defect existed
19 at the time the product left the manufacturer, and 3) the defect caused the plaintiff’s injury.”
20 Fyssakis v. Knight Equip. Corp., 826 P.2d 570, 571 (Nev. 1992).
21

I grant Golden’s motion for summary judgment on Speranza’s strict products liability

22 claim because Golden has pointed to a lack of evidence that its scooters contained a defect or

4. I grant Golden summary judgment on Speranza’s business defamation claim

14

“To succeed in a claim for business disparagement, the plaintiff must prove: (1) a false

15 and disparaging statement, (2) the unprivileged publication by the defendant, (3) malice, and
16 (4) special damages.” Clark Cnty. Sch. Dist. v. Virtual Educ. Software, Inc., 213 P.3d 496, 504
17 (Nev. 2009). I grant summary judgment in Golden’s favor on this claim because Speranza
18 admitted that Golden “never made any statements about [his] business.” ECF No. 17-3 at 8.
