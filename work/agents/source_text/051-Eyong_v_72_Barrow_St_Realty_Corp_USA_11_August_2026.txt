Opinion
PER CURIAM
*1 In the Circuit Court for Montgomery County, Simon
Eyong, representing himself, filed suit against his landlord,
72 Barrow St. Realty Corp. (“72 Barrow”), asserting claims
for negligence and negligent misrepresentation and seeking
$2 million in damages. He alleged that he leased an apartment
from 72 Barrow; that “sewer” water leaked into his bedroom
closet on August 29, 2024, damaging his clothing; that 72
Barrow and its agents negligently caused that leak; and that
72 Barrow, through its agents, negligently failed to disclose
its prior knowledge of an existing defect in the apartment,
inducing him to enter into the lease.
The case was tried by the court over one day. Mr. Eyong
introduced into evidence the residential lease agreement
executed November 1, 2023; letters he wrote to 72 Barrow's
managing partner, Scott Fixell, in the days after the alleged
leak; and photographs of stained clothing and damage inside
a closet. He testified and called four witnesses, including his
roommate and Mr. Fixell.
At the close of Mr. Eyong's case, the court entered judgment in
favor of 72 Barrow under Md. Rule 2-519(b), which permits
the court to “proceed, as the trier of fact, to determine the

facts and to render judgment against the plaintiff[.]” On the
negligence count, the court ruled that because the parties
entered into a lease, any negligent failure to maintain the
premises or to repair the leak was governed by that contract
and did not give rise to a tort duty. See Mesmer v. Md. Auto.
Ins. Fund, 353 Md. 241, 254 (1999) (“Under Maryland law,
negligent breach of a contract, absent a duty or obligation
imposed by a source independent of that arising out of the
contract itself, is not enough to sustain an action sounding in
tort” (citation omitted)). On the negligent misrepresentation
count, the court found on the evidence that Mr. Eyong did not
establish the existence of defective condition at the apartment
at the time the lease agreement was executed, emphasizing
the “lag time” of almost ten months between the execution
of the lease and the alleged leak, and consequently that there
was no negligent omission. Alternatively, as to both counts,
the court ruled that Mr. Eyong presented no evidence of the
value of the clothing he claimed was damaged when the leak
occurred, which was fatal to his claims. Mr. Eyong appeals
from that judgment.1
First, Mr. Eyong argues that the trial judge lacked sufficient
knowledge about the underlying facts of the case prior to
trial. This contention is unpreserved for our review because
Mr. Eyong did not raise it before the trial court. See Md.
Rule 8-131(a) (“Ordinarily, an appellate court will not decide
any [non-jurisdictional] issue unless it plainly appears by the
record to have been raised in or decided by the trial court[.]”).
In any event, the trial court, as the finder of fact in this case,
was not obligated to be familiar with the facts before the
evidence was presented to it and Mr. Eyong cites no legal
authority to the contrary. See Benway v. Md. Port Admin., 191
Md. App. 22, 32 (2010) (explaining that it is not an appellate
court's “function to seek out the law in support of a party's
appellate contentions” (citation omitted)).
*2 Equally unpreserved is Mr. Eyong's contention that the
trial court erred in its application of the rule on witnesses
because his roommate was made to leave the courtroom,
but Mr. Fixel was permitted to remain. Even if preserved,
Mr. Fixell was permitted to remain as 72 Barrow's corporate
designee. See Md. Rule 5-615(b)(2) (stating that “an officer ...
of a party that is not a natural person designated as its
representative by its attorney” shall not be excluded from the
courtroom).
Mr. Eyong next challenges comments made by the trial court
when considering a defense motion in limine relating to the
necessity of expert testimony on certain issues and comments

On the merits, Mr. Eyong contends that the trial court erred
by granting judgment in favor of 72 Barrow on both counts
because it ignored the evidence he presented. It is not our
role to reweigh the evidence or make our own credibility
determinations when reviewing the findings in a bench trial. It
is for the trial court to assess the credibility of witnesses as it is
“most aptly situated” and is “entitled to accept – or reject – all,
part, or none of their testimony, whether that testimony was or
was not contradicted or corroborated by any other evidence.”
Hripunovs v. Maximova, 263 Md. App. 244, 263, 269 (2024)
(cleaned up)). See also Md. Rule 8-131(c) (providing that
“[w]hen an action has been tried without a jury, an appellate
court will review the case on both the law and the evidence”).
Our review of the record reveals that the court's findings of
fact were supported by competent evidence in the record and,
thus, were not clearly erroneous and that the court did not err
in its application of the law to those facts.

72 Barrow has moved for sanctions against Mr. Eyong under
Rules 1-341 and 8-504, arguing that his citation to fictional
legal authority in his opening brief and his reply brief; the lack
of legal authority for his positions; and his failure to prepare
a record extract justify an award of reasonable attorneys’
fees and costs. On remand, the circuit court may entertain
a motion under Rule 1-341(a) concerning fees incurred by
72 Barrow in defending this appeal. See Litty v. Becker, 104
Md. App. 370, 376-78 (1995) (observing that a court may
entertain a motion for sanctions under Rule 1-341, including
reasonable attorneys’ fees, even after a judgment has been
entered, appealed, and the appeal concluded).
JUDGMENT OF THE CIRCUIT COURT FOR
MONTGOMERY COUNTY AFFIRMED. COSTS TO
BE PAID BY APPELLANT.
