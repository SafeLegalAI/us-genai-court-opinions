I. INTRODUCTION
The very first defense filings in this wrongful-death matter ask the Court to dismiss
the case with prejudice on fabricated authority. In lieu of answering the Complaint,
Dr. Didyuk, Dr. Gopal, and Methodist Healthcare filed Motions to Dismiss asserting
purported deficiencies in Plaintiff’s pre-suit notice. All three motions are frivolous, both
factually and legally, and are the subject of forthcoming Rule 11 notices. But two of the
motions go further: they are saturated with invented statutes, made-up appellate quotes,
fabricated standards, and rank mischaracterizations of holdings; all of which bear the
unmistakable fingerprint of unsupervised, generative AI.
Both the Didyuk Motion to Dismiss and the Methodist Motion to Dismiss request
money under “Tenn. Code Ann. § 20-5-119(c).” There is no such statute. There isn’t
even such a section. The chapter ends at § 20-5-118, which itself is a law regarding
automobile liability insurance carriers that has no discernible relevance to this health care
liability case.
Methodist’s motion rests its only substantive ground on “T.C.A. § 29-26-121
(a)(1)(e),” a subdivision that does not exist. There is also no preceding (a)(1)(a), (b),
(c), or (d).
The Didyuk memorandum relies on a 46-word quotation attributed to the Supreme
Court of Tennessee in Martin v. Rolling Hills Hospital, LLC, “quoting” Stevens v.
Hickman Community Health Care Services, Inc. for its central argument. Neither
opinion contains the invented passage.
The Methodist memorandum instructs the Court that the plaintiff must overcome
“the presumption of prejudice set forth above,” citing page 335 of Martin. Page 335
contains no such statement, nor does the larger Martin opinion, which granted

Any one of these errors, in isolation, might be unremarkable. Where Didyuk
identified a case as “AMIBU” instead of “AMISUB” could be a slipped keystroke. Where
Methodist called the plaintiff “Shaw for Estate of Tuttle” instead of the correct “Estate of
John Suttle” could be a momentary lapse of concentration. Where both firms repeatedly
change the tense of operative verbs in quoted authority, “owe” for “owed”, for example,
might be a misread. The problem is not any one error. It is the population of errors:
dozens of instances that sort into classes no combination of slipped keys can produce, that
repeat identically across two firms’ filings, and that slant the law, without exception, in
Defendants’ favor.
The specific pattern at work here matches the documented behavior of generative
artificial intelligence systems. A large language model does not copy text from sources. It
4

generates text one word at a time, selecting each next word by statistical probability given
the words around it. Substitution is therefore not a malfunction of these systems; it is how
they fundamentally operate by design.
When AI is asked to reproduce a quotation, the model reconstructs it, and the
reconstruction swaps synonyms (“that” for “which”), regularizes grammar (“owe” for
“owed”), strengthens qualifiers (“should” becomes “will,” an illustration becomes a rule),
and fudges on exactly the numbers and identifiers that carry precision in the legal world:
volume numbers, docket digits, subsection letters, pin pages, and party names, each
pulled toward a statistically similar neighbor.
That is how a real case comes to bear another real case’s reporter citation. That is
how “each other provider being sent a notice” becomes simply “each other,” how
“sufficient to enable defendants to obtain and review” becomes truncated to “sufficient to
obtain and review,” and how a statutory chapter that ends at section 20-5-118 acquires a
section 20-5-119. And because the system’s output is designed to look and read correct;
every one of these fabrications is presented in confident, grammatically-proper,
formatted prose.
That is the difference between honest human mistake and the likely-AI generated
content filed by these Defendants. Human error occurs randomly: it misspells words,
garbles punctuation marks, and it works against the drafter’s interest as often as it does
for it. The errors in Defendants’ filings do none of those things. They present error as fact,
in beautifully worded language, and always sycophantically tilted toward the party who
prompted the AI.
And this is what makes unsupervised AI drafting particularly dangerous in the
legal setting. Law derives meaning at the level of the single word, letter, and
5

digit: the difference between “owed” and “owe” is the difference between what one court
held about the singular parties before it and a standing rule for every case after it, and the
difference between 467 S.W.3d 911 and 523 S.W.3d 619 is the difference between one case
and another.
A tool, generative AI, whose default mode is substitution fails at precisely the
places where the law derives meaning, and it defeats the ordinary safeguard against
human error, which is reading for sense, because every substituted word does, in fact,
make sense.
The only review that catches AI-drafting error is the one that Rule 11.02 has always
required: actually reading the cited authority and verifying it. The Didyuk and Methodist
filings are what occurs when that review does not happen. And how these papers were in
fact researched, drafted, reviewed, and put in front of this Court is a question only their
signers can answer, under oath.
Under Rule 11.03(1)(b), the Court may enter an order describing the specific
conduct that appears to violate Rule 11.02 and direct the signers of these motions to show
cause. The attached proposed order does that and no more: it recites the verified instances
with particularity, directs sworn written responses within 21 days, requires disclosure of
how these filings were researched and drafted, and sets a hearing. It presumes nothing,
adjudicates nothing, reserves all questions of sanction, and gives Defense Counsel every
opportunity to explain themselves. Plaintiff asks the Court to enter that order.

III. LEGAL STANDARD
Rule 11.02 provides that by “signing, filing, submitting, or later advocating” a
paper, an attorney certifies “that to the best of the person’s knowledge, information, and
belief, formed after an inquiry reasonable under the circumstances,” the paper “is not
being presented for any improper purpose,” its “claims, defenses, and other legal
contentions therein are warranted by existing law or by a nonfrivolous argument for the
extension, modification, or reversal of existing law or the establishment of new law,” and
its factual contentions have or are likely to have evidentiary support. Tenn. R. Civ. P.
11.02.
Under Rule 11.03, a party may move for sanctions, separately, with a 21-day safe
harbor. Tenn. R. Civ. P. 11.03(1)(a). Or, “[o]n its own initiative, the court may enter an
order describing the specific conduct that appears to violate subdivision 11.02 and
directing an attorney, law firm, or party to show cause why it has not violated subdivision
11.02 with respect thereto.” Tenn. R. Civ. P. 11.03(1)(b).
This motion seeks the second path. The threshold for that second path is conduct
that “appears to violate” Rule 11.02. Sanctions, if any ever issue, are “limited to what is
sufficient to deter repetition,” monetary sanctions may not be awarded against a
represented party for legal-contention violations, Tenn. R. Civ. P. 11.03(2)(a), and
monetary sanctions on the Court’s initiative require that the show-cause order issue
“before a voluntary dismissal or settlement of the claims made by or against the party
which is, or whose attorneys are, to be sanctioned,” Tenn. R. Civ. P. 11.03(2)(b). Any

16

sanction order must “describe the conduct determined to constitute a violation of this rule
and explain the basis for the sanction imposed.” Tenn. R. Civ. P. 11.03(3).
The test under Rule 11 “is one of objective reasonableness under all the
circumstances.” Andrews v. Bible, 812 S.W.2d 284, 288 (Tenn. 1991). Andrews adopted
the checklist that answers most of what this motion presents: “An attorney must READ
every paper before signing it,” he “must make a reasonable prefiling investigation of the
FACTS,” he “must research the LAW, unless he is certain he knows it,” and “[a] pure heart
but an empty head is of no avail.” Id. (quoting Whittington v. Ohio River Co., 115 F.R.D.
201 (E.D. Ky. 1987)) (capitalization in original).
Independent of Rule 11, “the courts of this state have, under the inherent power to
supervise and control their own proceedings, the authority to sanction attorneys, but only
for pursuing matters in bad faith or conducting themselves in a reckless manner,”
Andrews, 812 S.W.2d at 291. A person acts recklessly “when the person is aware of, but
consciously disregards, a substantial and unjustifiable risk of such a nature that its
disregard constitutes a gross deviation from the standard of care that an ordinary person
would exercise under all the circumstances,” Hodges v. S.C. Toof & Co., 833 S.W.2d 896,
901 (Tenn. 1992). It may be that, given their extensive public proclamations on the use
of AI in the practice of law, the lawyers and law firms acted with a conscious disregard of
its known risks.
IV. ARGUMENT
Rule 11.02 makes every signature on a filing a personal certification to the Court
that the paper’s legal contentions are “warranted by existing law,” formed “after an
inquiry reasonable under the circumstances.” Tenn. R. Civ. P. 11.02. The certification is
personal by design: “The purpose of Rule 11 as a whole is to bring home to the individual
17

signer his personal, nondelegable responsibility,” an obligation that “is not a team effort
but in the last analysis, yours alone.” Andrews, 812 S.W.2d at 288. The final responsibility
rests with the signer because courts rule on the law as counsel presents it, and no judge
can re-run counsel’s research for every filing.
A false certification injures the Court itself. “Baseless filing puts the machinery of
justice in motion, burdening courts and individuals alike with needless inconvenience,
expense, and delay,” Andrews, 812 S.W.2d at 292, and when the burden goes unnoticed
the falsehood passes into an order and speaks as the Court.
A.

Every ruling this Court makes rests on counsel’s representations
about the law, and those representations reach into the Court’s own
orders.
Courts run on the premise that lawyers and law firms are candid, honest, and

accurate when they cite authority in support of their positions. As the Sixth Circuit stated
this spring when it sanctioned two Tennessee lawyers for fabricated citations and
misrepresentations, the integrity of proceedings depends on the premise that “lawyers
and courts alike are able to rely on one another’s representations.” Whiting v. City of
Athens (6th Cir. Mar. 13, 2026) citing Azar v. Garza, 584 U.S. 726, 730 (2018). No judge
has the hours to pull and read every authority in every brief. In Whiting, counsel’s
unreliable filings “forced the court to individually verify every single citation,” and the
panel counted that burden in setting the sanction. The certification required by Rule 11
exists so that courts are not forced to do that.
In Tennessee trial practice, the reliance goes one step further: prevailing counsel
routinely draft the orders that courts sign. The Supreme Court has approved the practice
within limits, in a Shelby County case: “it is permissible and indeed sometimes desirable

18

for the trial court to permit counsel for any party to submit proposed findings and
conclusions…”. Smith v. UHS of Lakeside, Inc., 439 S.W.3d 303 (Tenn. 2014).
Thus, the path from a defective brief to a defective order is short and ordinary: the
briefing’s authorities go into the proposed order, and the proposed order, once signed,
speaks as the Court. From there it circulates: it is cited back to the same court and to other
courts as authority, and it enters the published record on which lawyers, and the research
tools lawyers increasingly use, rely.
This record in this case already shows how that cycle runs. On July 16, one firm
filed an invented prejudice rule from the Lawson case. On July 17, a second firm filed the
same invented sentence, word for word, as settled law. On July 20, a third firm’s
memorandum reproduced a defective passage from the first filing character for character
and adopted both earlier filings by reference. Copying without reading is how one error
becomes three filings. An order is the next stop. And here, there is every indication that
this type of misconduct will proliferate, in this very case, if the Court does not step in to
put a stop to it now while these fabricated authorities are still confined to Defense
Counsel’s motions and not in any paper bearing this Court’s signature.
B.

The conduct described appears to violate Rule 11.02, which is the only
threshold Rule 11.03(1)(b) sets.
Rule 11.03(1)(b) asks whether specific conduct “appears to violate” Rule 11.02’s

certification, made “after an inquiry reasonable under the circumstances.” The answer
here is an unqualified yes. This motion and the review by undersigned counsel of
Defendants’ citations constitute a reasonable, indeed, thorough, inquiry. The Defendants’
submission of invented, fabricated, and mischaracterized authority to this Court as real

19

(and further seek dismissal of this case on that bogus authority) represents conduct that
appears to violate Rule 11.02.
These Defendants made three requests to this Court for money under a statute that
does not exist; they presented a motion they state is based on a subdivision that is not in
the statute; and they cite pages that are not in the opinions they name. These errors
cannot occur where Defense Counsel has done the bare minimum of reading the very
authority they present to the court.
Quotations that no court wrote also fail the Rule 11 certification. The invented 46word passage attributed to Martin and the mash-up quote ending with “relevant medical
chart” attributed to Stevens could have each been dispelled by reading the single page
cited for them. The same is true of the other misquotes inventoried above.
Invented standards presented as law are not “warranted by existing law” in any
sense of Rule 11.02(2), and the Defendants do not present their positions as good faith
argument for extending the law; but rather each is falsely presented as what the law
already is. This is violative of Rule 11 as well.
C.

Two explanations fit the record; the Court need not choose between
them because both require sworn answers.
There are two possible explanations for Defendants’ submissions. The first

explanation is that these filings contain the output of unsupervised and unreviewed
generative-AI. Certainly the fabrications and defects discussed above carry all the
signatures of AI documented in reported decisions: authority invented with a plausiblelooking number in a plausible-looking place (a fee provision one section past the end of a
real chapter; a subdivision spliced from two real ones); quotations in a court’s cadence
the court never wrote; standards phrased in the idiom of real doctrine; a real case bearing

20

another real case’s reporter citation; the same invented text surfacing in two firms’ filings
a day apart; quotation defects inherited through chains of copying in which no one opened
the original; and errors that uniformly favor the filer.
Moreover, this is a known and, regrettably, exploding problem in the practice of
law, with the Sixth Circuit, the Western District of Tennessee, and the Tennessee Court of
Appeals each having confronted its consequences in the past eighteen months. See, e.g.,
Whiting, slip op.; United States v. Farris, No. 25-5623 (6th Cir. Apr. 3, 2026) (published);
Reaves, No. 2:25-cv-02623 (W.D. Tenn. June 2, 2026).
The second explanation is that lawyers fabricated authority and quotations
knowingly. Nothing in this motion asserts that, Plaintiff’s Counsel does not believe that is
what occurred, nor does Plaintiff believe the record reflects it. The first explanation is the
more probable reading of the documented pattern. But the second cannot be excluded
without hearing from the drafters.
Whether the fabrications were intentional or the result of unsupervised AI dictates
only the consequences, not whether the Show Cause Order should be entered. If the origin
was unverified machine output, the certification was signed without the reasonable
inquiry Rule 11.02 requires, and the response to being caught becomes the determining
factor. Indeed, all of the reported cases significantly weigh any sanctions against candor
with attorneys who promptly explain and take responsibility for their actions rarely facing
any punitive measure.
D.

The Court should enter the Show Cause Order now.
This is case is at its outset. These are the first substantive defense filings. The

fabrications included in the Didyuk and Methodist filings have not yet been argued at a
hearing, incorporated into a proposed order, or relied on in a ruling. Entry now protects
21

every later stage. The record and the public statements of both the Starnes Davis and
Baker Donelson firms confirm that they are using, and will, in all likelihood, continue to
use, in this case, generative AI for their research and filings. The guardrails for that use,
and its consequences on Plaintiff (whose Counsel has spent an enormous amount of time
verifying opposing counsel’s citations) and the Court, should be clearly set forth now.
The proposed order is calibrated to fairness. It describes the conduct with the
particularity Rule 11.03(3) contemplates. It directs answers under oath within 21 days, on
a verification protocol this district’s federal court has already used in Reaves. It requires
production of drafts and identification of resources, sets an evidentiary hearing, and
reserves every question of consequence for after the answers. It draws no inference about
origin, states that no sanction against any represented party is contemplated, and
adjudicates nothing.
At this stage it is up to Defense Counsel to fully and forthrightly account for the
conduct described above.
