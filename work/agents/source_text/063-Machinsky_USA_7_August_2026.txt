13    In Debral Realty, the Supreme Judicial Court explained that the statutory scheme was adopted to ameliorate
      the “harsh effects” of the common law doctrine of lis pendens on unsuspecting “good faith” purchasers. 383
      Mass. at 560-61. Under the common law, “the mere existence of litigation involving title to real property was
      deemed constructive notice to the world, so that anyone who purchased the disputed property while the
      suit was pending was bound by the judgment ultimately rendered.” Id. at 560. Now, under the statutes, “the
      judgment of the court in litigation affecting real property is no longer binding on those acquiring an interest in
      the property unless a memorandum of lis pendens was filed in the registry of deeds before the acquisition.” Id.
      at 561. The statutory scheme thus limits the “scope of the common law rule” without changing its effect. Id. “By
      putting anyone interested in real estate that is in dispute on notice of the dispute, the statute ensures that a
      prospective third-party transferee can, with the exercise of reasonable prudence, acquire information relevant
      to a decision whether to consummate the transaction.” Id. at 561-62. Indeed a “memorandum of lis pendens
      does nothing that a conscientious seller of property would not do as a matter of course.... [C]onsiderations of
      fairness would ordinarily prompt the seller of property that is the subject of a legal dispute to inform potential
      buyers or mortgagees of the existence of that dispute. A notice of lis pendens does just that.” Id. at 566.
