                      MEMORANDUM DECISION

Vice Chief Judge Eppich authored the decision of the Court, in which
Presiding Judge Vásquez and Chief Judge Staring concurred.


E P P I C H, Vice Chief Judge:

               In this family law matter, Brently Easten appeals from the trial
court’s award of attorney fees against him for having taken unreasonable
positions with respect to child support and failing to produce records
relating to his marijuana use. He contends that the court erred in failing to
explain its methodology in arriving at the fee award or expressly identify
those fees that were incurred due to his unreasonable conduct. For the
following reasons, we affirm.

              In the proceedings below, Easten maintained that he should
not have to pay the requested $114 a month in child support because he and
Appellee Fairrow were exercising equal parenting time. He did so despite
having been informed that parenting time was not the only factor
considered in the child support calculation and that income disparity
played a role as well. Easten stuck to his position, even after a settlement
judge and the trial judge both cautioned him that doing so could result in
an attorney fee award. Easten himself had acknowledged that it was
“ridiculous” to go to trial over such a small amount but chose to do so.
Easten also failed to cooperate in providing records of his marijuana use.
As a result, Fairrow unnecessarily incurred legal fees to obtain the records
directly from Easten’s marijuana dispensary.          In light of Easten’s
recalcitrance with respect to these two issues, the trial court indicated it
would assess a portion of Fairrow’s attorney fees against Easten.

             Fairrow submitted a China Doll affidavit seeking an award of
$28,612.66. Easten did not file a response or objection to the affidavit. The
trial court awarded Fairrow $22,766.60. Easten filed a motion for
reconsideration, arguing that the award was unfair and complaining about
the lack of an explanation as to why the fees were awarded. The court
summarily denied the motion.

              On appeal, Easten contests the trial court’s failure to include
detailed findings of fact or to adequately explain the basis for its
calculations in its decision. But, as Easten concedes, such findings as to fees

awarded under A.R.S. § 25-324 are only required if requested by a party.
See Myrick v. Maloney, 235 Ariz. 491, ¶ 10 (App. 2014) (party cannot
challenge lack of findings under § 25-324 when none have been requested).
Easten does not assert he made such a request, but nevertheless argues that
the lack of findings precludes meaningful appellate review, thereby
denying him due process.1 But the legal authorities cited by Easten do not
support his argument.

              One such authority is Napp v. Napp, which Easten cites for the
proposition that an award must be supported by a record sufficient to
permit meaningful review. But that case does not exist—at least not at the
citation provided by Easten. We suspect he intended to cite Breitbart-Napp
v. Napp, 216 Ariz. 74 (App. 2007). In that case we vacated the trial court’s
fee award because of the stale nature of the financial information upon
which it was based and the court’s apparent decision to make the award
based upon which party prevailed on the merits, an improper basis under
§ 25-324. Breitbart-Napp, 216 Ariz. 74, ¶ 39. Neither of those considerations
apply here.

               Appels-Meehan v. Appels, 167 Ariz. 182 (App. 1991), upon
which Easten also relies, did not involve a review of a trial court’s award of
attorney fees. Rather, we declined to award fees on appeal based upon a lack
of information regarding the financial circumstances of the parties, the trial
court having received no evidence regarding finances. Id. at 185. Here, by
contrast, the trial court noted a small disparity in the parties’ finances.2

               Moreover, contrary to Easten’s claim, the record here is
sufficient to permit him to seek meaningful review. Specifically, in support
of his argument that the fee award necessarily included amounts unrelated
to the unreasonableness of his positions in the trial court, he could have
identified specific billing entries in the China Doll affidavit that were
unrelated to his conduct and offered a calculation as to whether the
remaining entries were sufficient to support the sum awarded. By failing
to do so, he has waived the issue on appeal. See Ramos v. Nichols, 252 Ariz.
519, ¶¶ 8–11 (App. 2022) (failure to adequately cite record or develop
arguments results in waiver).

              Fairrow seeks attorney fees on appeal under Rule 25, Ariz. R.
Civ. App. P., as a sanction against Easten for pursuing a frivolous appeal.
Although Easten’s arguments are without merit, we do not find them
frivolous and therefore decline to award fees on that basis. In our
discretion, we decline Fairrow’s alternative request for a fee award under
§ 25-324. However, as the prevailing party Fairrow is entitled to her
reasonable costs on appeal upon compliance with Rule 21(b), Ariz. R. Civ.
