In considering a motion to dismiss, I take all well-pleaded allegations of material fact as

8 true and construe them in a light most favorable to the non-moving party. Kwan v. SanMedica
9 Int’l, 854 F.3d 1088, 1096 (9th Cir. 2017). However, I do not “assume the truth of legal
10 conclusions merely because they are cast in the form of factual allegations.” Navajo Nation v.
11 Dep’t of the Interior, 876 F.3d 1144, 1163 (9th Cir. 2017).
12

To withstand a motion to dismiss, a complaint must make sufficient factual allegations to

13 establish a plausible entitlement to relief. Bell Atl. Corp. v. Twombly, 550 U.S. 544, 556 (2007).
14 Such allegations must amount to “more than labels and conclusions, [or] a formulaic recitation of
15 the elements of a cause of action.” Id. at 555. Instead, the complaint must include “a short and
16 plain statement of the claim” that shows the plaintiff “is entitled to relief” and gives the
17 defendants “fair notice of what the claim is and the grounds upon which it rests.” Id. (simplified).
18

I must “freely give leave” to amend “when justice so requires.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a)(2).

B. Kelly fails to state a claim under SEC Rule 10b-5.
Kelly alleges that FINRA violated SEC Rule 10b-5 when it knowingly made false and

3 misleading statements regarding the MMTLP trading halt and related corporate-action notice.
4 Specifically, he alleges that FINRA falsely suggested that its actions regarding MMTLP were
5 authorized by the SEC and that there would be no trading halt when FINRA already decided it
6 was going to issue one. Kelly relied on these representations when determining “how to act
7 regarding [his] MMTLP holdings” and later was unable to liquidate or transfer the shares. ECF
8 No. 42 at 17-18. FINRA argues that Kelly fails to state a claim for a Rule 10b-5 violation
9 because he does not allege that he purchased or sold MMTLP shares because of FINRA’s
10 announcements or actions.
11

Rule 10b-5 makes it unlawful to “employ any device, scheme, or artifice to defraud,” or

12 to “engage in any act, practice, or course of business which operates or would operate as a fraud
13 or deceit upon any person . . . in connection with the purchase or sale of any security.” 17 C.F.R.
14 § 240.10b-5. The requirement to “establish fraud ‘in connection with the purchase or sale of any
15 security’ . . . actually contains two separate elements: ‘in connection with’ and a ‘purchase or
16 sale.’” Roberts v. Peat, Marwick, Mitchell & Co., 857 F.2d 646, 650 (9th Cir. 1988). “The
17 former requires a causal relationship between the fraud and the resulting injury. The latter . . .
18 requires an actual purchase or sale of securities, or a contract to purchase or sell.” Id.
19 (simplified). Shareholders who “decided not to sell their shares” due to the alleged fraud cannot
20 maintain a Rule 10b-5 action. Blue Chip Stamps v. Manor Drug Stores, 421 U.S. 723, 737-38,
21 749 (1975).
22

Kelly does not allege that he purchased, sold, or entered into a contract to purchase or sell

23 MMTLP shares due to FINRA’s actions. He already held the shares when FINRA revised the

4

1 MMTLP corporate-action notice. The fact that FINRA’s actions may have “influenced” other
2 investors’ trading decisions about their MMTLP shares does not allow Kelly to bring a Rule 10b3 5 claim against FINRA. See ECF No. 56 at 27. I therefore dismiss this claim. See Williams v.
4 Sinclair, 529 F.2d 1383, 1389 (9th Cir. 1975) (holding that shareholders could not maintain a
5 Rule 10b-5 claim when they held “shares prior to” the alleged fraud and “retained those shares
6 after” the fraud “without making any further purchase or sales”).

C. Kelly does not state a claim that FINRA violated the Exchange Act.
Kelly next alleges that FINRA violated several provisions of, and rules promulgated

9 under, the Exchange Act. FINRA argues that those provisions do not create a private right of
10 action and Kelly fails to state a claim for them. FINRA is correct, so I dismiss this claim.
11

The first provision Kelly alleges that FINRA violated is 15 U.S.C. § 78o-3(b)(6), which

12 sets out the purposes behind registering national securities associations. But § 78o-3(b)(6) does
13 not by itself create a private cause of action. Shahmirzadi v. Smith Barney, Harris Upham & Co.,
14 636 F. Supp. 49, 52 (D.D.C. 1985).
15

The SAC then lists three regulations that FINRA violated: 17 C.F.R. §§ 240.15c2-11,

16 240.17a-4, and 242.203(b)(3). FINRA argues that these rules apply exclusively to broker-dealers
17 and other market participants and not to self-regulating organizations like FINRA. Kelly now
18 agrees that these regulations do not apply to FINRA, so he fails to state a claim that FINRA
19 violated them. ECF No. 56 at 18.
20

In his response, Kelly for the first time cites two other provisions of the Exchange Act

21 that FINRA allegedly violated. This argument fails for two reasons. First, a plaintiff cannot
22 amend his complaint through briefing. Broam v. Bogan, 320 F.3d 1023, 1026 n.2 (9th Cir. 2003).
23 Second, even if I were to consider these allegations as properly pleaded, neither provision creates

5

1 a private cause of action. The first new provision Kelly argues FINRA violated is 17 C.F.R.
2 § 240.17a-1, which requires FINRA to preserve certain records. Section 240.17a-1 was
3 promulgated under 15 U.S.C. § 78q(a), and the Supreme Court held that § 78q(a) does not
4 “create a private cause of action in favor of anyone.” Touche Ross & Co. v. Redington, 442 U.S.
5 560, 569 (1979); Carapico v. Philadelphia Stock Exch., No. Civ. A. 93-3066, 1994 WL 50295, at
6 *2 (E.D. Pa. Feb. 14, 1994), aff’d, 43 F.3d 1460 (3d Cir. 1994). Next, Kelly cites 15 U.S.C.
7 § 78s(g)(1) to argue that FINRA failed to comply with its own rules. ECF No. 56 at 18. But
8 there is no private right of action against FINRA for violating its own rules. Kelly, 2025 WL
9 2962788, at *4 (citing Sparta Surgical Corp. v. Nat’l Ass’n of Sec. Dealers, Inc., 159 F.3d 1209,
10 1213 (9th Cir. 1998)).

D. I dismiss Kelly’s Fifth Amendment claim for lack of personal jurisdiction.
Kelly next alleges that FINRA’s trading halt violated his procedural due process rights by

13 depriving him of his right to control his securities without sufficient process. Kelly raised this
14 same claim in his previous complaint. ECF No. 7 at 8-9. I dismissed that claim because I lacked
15 personal jurisdiction. Kelly, 2025 WL 2962788, at *2-3, 5.
16

In its motion to dismiss the SAC, FINRA “reserve[d] its argument that the SAC is subject

17 to dismissal because [I] lack personal jurisdiction over FINRA for the reasons discussed” in its
18 motion to dismiss Kelly’s previous complaint. ECF No. 47 at 3 n.3. I lack personal jurisdiction
19 over FINRA for Kelly’s Fifth Amendment claim for the same reasons I did in his previous
20 complaint. FINRA is not subject to general jurisdiction in Nevada. Kelly, 2025 WL 2962788, at
21 *2. Kelly has not pleaded sufficient facts in his SAC that FINRA targeted its actions at Nevada
22 or had minimum contacts with Nevada to establish specific personal jurisdiction. Id. at *3. And I
23 dismissed his only claims arising under the Exchange Act, so I may not assert pendent personal

6

1 jurisdiction over his Fifth Amendment claim. Id. at *5 (citing Action Embroidery Corp. v. Atl.
2 Embroidery, Inc., 368 F.3d 1174, 1180 (9th Cir. 2004)). Even if I could assert pendent personal
3 jurisdiction, I would decline to do so. Id. Therefore, I dismiss Kelly’s Fifth Amendment claim
4 without prejudice.

E. I dismiss Kelly’s claim for declaratory and injunctive relief.
Kelly raised a separate claim for declaratory and injunctive relief in his SAC. He

7 requests that I declare that FINRA’s conduct regarding MMTLP shares violated the Exchange
8 Act and the relevant SEC rules. And he seeks injunctive relief relating to FINRA’s
9 communications of the MMTLP shares, its handling of corporate-action data, and its future
10 trading halts. Because I have dismissed all his other claims, including his claims that FINRA
11 violated the Exchange Act and SEC rules, and because I do not have personal jurisdiction over
12 the constitutional claim, he has no viable underlying claim to support these forms of relief in this
13 case. So I also dismiss this claim.

G. I warn Kelly about using generative AI in the future.
FINRA states that Kelly’s opposition to its motion to dismiss contains several nonexistent

20 case citations and quotations that do not appear in Kelly’s cited caselaw. I reviewed the citations
21 in the first few pages of Kelly’s opposition and found several quotes that did not appear in the
22 cited cases. These false quotes appear on page 2, lines 11-12; page 3, lines 3-4; and page 9, line
23 20 to page 10, line 5 of Kelly’s opposition. ECF No. 56 at 2-3, 9-10.

7

1

When a party presents a filing to a court, the party certifies “that to the best of the

2 [party’s] knowledge, information, and belief, formed after an inquiry reasonable under the
3 circumstances . . . the claims, defenses, and other legal contentions are warranted by existing
4 law.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 11(b). This imposes an “affirmative duty of investigation . . . as to law . . .
5 before motions are filed.” Golden Eagle Distrib. Corp., v. Burroughs Corp., 801 F.2d 1531,
6 1536 (9th Cir. 1986). This extends to attorneys and pro se litigants alike. Fed. R. Civ. P. 11(b).
7 “A fake opinion is not existing law, and citation to a fake opinion does not provide a non8 frivolous ground for extending, modifying, or reversing existing law, or for establishing new
9 law. An attempt to persuade a court or oppose an adversary by relying on fake opinions is an
10 abuse of the adversary system.” Whiting v. City of Athens, Tenn., 170 F.4th 455, 461 (6th Cir.
11 Mar. 13, 2026) (quotations omitted).
12

There has been a rise in fake authority cited in briefs, usually as the result of using

13 generative AI software, like ChatGPT, to draft pleadings. Chavez-DeRemer v. NAB, LLC, No.
14 2:21-CV-00984-JAD-EJY, 2025 WL 2308676, at *3 (D. Nev. Aug. 11, 2025). Generative AI
15 often invents fake cases and legal precedent in its drafting, and using it is no excuse for not
16 verifying the veracity of citations. See Whiting, 170 F.4th at 461 (“Citing even a single fake case
17 can be sanctionable because no brief, pleading, motion, or any other paper filed in any court
18 should contain any citations—whether provided by generative AI or any other source—that a
19 lawyer [or pro se party] has not personally read and verified.” (simplified)). Citing fake legal
20 authority is not harmless. It wastes the other parties’ and the court’s resources trying to track
21 down the nonexistent cases. Id. at 467 (“Citing fake cases unnecessarily burdens the court and
22 the taxpayers, so courts can and should fine the offending lawyers to reimburse the court for its
23 time.” (simplified)). And it “can harm the reputation of judges and courts whose names are

8

1 falsely invoked as authors of the bogus opinions and the reputation of a party attributed with
2 fictional conduct.” Id. at 461-62 (simplified).
3

Going forward, Kelly is reminded of his duty under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure

4 11(b)(2) and that citing fake cases drafted by generative AI violates this rule. Failure to comply
5 in the future may result in sanctions, such as an order to pay a penalty into court or nonmonetary
6 sanctions, such as striking claims or defenses. Fed. R. Civ. P. 11(c)(4).

THE PARTIES’ MOTIONS FOR JUDICIAL NOTICE

8

Both sides move for me to take judicial notice of various documents related to FINRA’s

9 trade halt of MMTLP shares. Kelly also moves for leave to file a surreply in support of his
10 motion for judicial notice. I looked at all of the attached documents, and I deny both motions for
11 judicial notice because the documents would not change the outcome of this order even if I
12 considered them. None of the documents shows that Kelly purchased, sold, or entered into a
13 contract to purchase or sell MMTLP shares due to FINRA’s conduct. Nor do they offer
14 sufficient facts to establish specific personal jurisdiction over FINRA in Nevada. Accordingly, I
15 deny the motions for judicial notice and Kelly’s motions for leave to file a surreply as moot.
