14      II.       LEGAL STANDARD

15   Federal Rule of Evidence 702 provides:

16                   A witness who is qualified as an expert by knowledge, skill,
                     experience, training, or education may testify in the form of an opinion
17                   or otherwise if the proponent demonstrates to the court that it is more
                     likely than not that:
18                           (a) the expert's scientific, technical, or other specialized
19                           knowledge will help the trier of fact to understand the evidence
                             or to determine a fact in issue;
20                           (b) the testimony is based on sufficient facts or data;
                             (c) the testimony is the product of reliable principles and
21                           methods; and
                             (d) the expert's opinion reflects a reliable application of the
22                           principles and methods to the facts of the case.
23   Experts who are “qualified . . . by knowledge, skill, experience, training, or education,” Fed.R.
24   Evid. 702, and whose opinions otherwise meet Rule 702’s requirements, may offer opinions “not
25   based on personal knowledge or observations.” Moreno v. Ross Island Sand & Gravel Co., 2015

 2   hearsay” for the basis of their opinion. Fannie Mae v. LaRuffa, 702 F. App’x 505, 507 (9th Cir.

 3   2017). Additionally, evidence that “could be presented in an admissible form at trial” may be

 8   admissible form for trial, it should not be excluded at summary judgment.” Id.

22   social media platforms. Plaintiffs respond that Dr. Klein phrased the question as he did to maintain

23   consistency with the survey’s other questions and thus avoid revealing the purpose of the survey,

24   which could bias responses.

 1           “[P]urported flaws in survey design . . . will usually go to the weight a jury accords the

 2   survey, not whether the jury can be shown it in the first place.” Johnson v. Nissan N. Am., Inc.,

 3   2022 WL 2869528, at *8 (N.D.Cal. 2022). Defendants offer no reason why Klein’s choice of

 4   phrasing “renders the survey unreliable as a matter of law, or why [it] [wa]s so wrong that the jury

 5   cannot be trusted to evaluate the merits of [their] objections.” Id. This objection is overruled.

 6           As to the second, and as described in the Court’s Order Denying Motion for Summary

 7   Judgment as to Breathitt school district (Dkt. No. 2728, “Breathitt MSJ Order”), plaintiffs may

 8   proceed on their theory that defendants’ negligent design of their platforms causes students’

 9   compulsive social media use which, in turn, results in costs to the school district. Dr. Klein’s

10   evidence speaks to the effects of students’ compulsive use of social media, which is critical to

11   plaintiffs’ theory; that it does not tease out causation as to each actionable feature does not bar

12   admissibility. See Primiano v. Cook, 598 F.3d 558, 565 (9th Cir. 2010) (“Reliable expert testimony

13   need only be relevant, and need not establish every element that the plaintiff must prove, in order

14   to be admissible”); In re Toyota Motor Corp. Unintended Acceleration Mktg., Sales Pracs., &

15   Prods. Liab. Litig., 978 F.Supp. 2d 1053, 1066–67 (C.D.Cal. 2013) (“[N]o single expert provides a

16   self-sufficient opinion that [defendants’ conduct] in fact caused the [plaintiff’s harm]. This is not

17   dispositive.”).

18           Returning to defendants’ “relevance” objection, the Court does not find that the two

19   challenges vitiate the probative value of the survey in its entirety. That defendants may cross

20   examine on these issues is valid, but the issue is one of weight, not admissibility. The motion on

21   this ground is denied.

22                     2.     Retrospective Reporting

23           Defendants next argue that Klein’s method of asking respondents to recall how many

24   minutes they spent dealing with social media years ago is unreliable, as people cannot recall such

25   details with precision. Plaintiffs respond that retrospective surveys are standard and accepted in

26   research literature outside of litigation, and that the Klein survey also included instructions not to

27   guess and a “don’t know” answer to avoid recall bias.

28

     Case 4:22-md-03047-YGR           Document 2750         Filed 02/17/26      Page 6 of 18




 1          Defendants’ cited authority fails to show that the Ninth Circuit prohibits admitting

 2   retrospective surveys. Indeed, one of defendants’ cited cases acknowledges that survey respondents

 3   “might reliably remember” certain retrospective events. In re: Autozone, Inc., 2016 WL 4208200,

 4   at *19 (N.D.Cal. 2016). Where the survey respondents, as with the teachers here, cannot be

 5   expected to have kept records of the relevant subject matter over many years, retrospective “survey

 6   evidence is relevant to give an approximation of damages.” Alcantar v. Hobart Serv., 2013 WL

 7   156530, at *2 (C.D.Cal. 2013) (unreasonable to expect employees to keep records of every meal

 8   break provided). That is the purpose for which plaintiffs offer the survey here. As above, these

 9   disputed methodological issues go to weight, not admissibility. This objection is overruled.

10                  3.      Lack of Representativeness and Hearsay Objections

11          Defendants argue that only a fraction of the districts’ teachers responded to Klein’s surveys

12   and that Klein has no way of knowing whether those who responded were representative of the full

13   population of teachers in the districts. The Third Edition of the Reference Manual on Scientific

14   Evidence describes high survey response rates as “desirable,” but nevertheless notes that

15   “surprisingly comparable results have been obtained in many surveys with varying response rates,

16   suggesting that surveys may achieve reasonable estimates even with relatively low response rates.”

17   Reference Manual on Scientific Evidence (3d ed.) (2011) at 384-85. Regardless, “any problems

18   with the response rate affect the weight, and not the admissibility of the study.” Alcantar, 2013 WL

19   156530, at *4. The Court declines to grant the motion on this ground.

20          Defendants further assert that Klein’s survey failed to sufficiently account for “non-

21   response bias,” which they claim is contrary to the Third Edition of the Reference Manual for

22   Scientific Evidence and grounds for exclusion. In general, critiques regarding such “technical

23   inadequacies,” including survey methodology, “bear on the weight of the survey, not its

24   admissibility." Fortune Dynamic, Inc. v. Victoria's Secret Stores Brand Management, Inc., 618

25   F.3d 1025, 1036 (9th Cir. 2010); see also Clicks Billiards, Inc. v. Sixshooters, Inc., 251 F.3d 1252,

26   1263–64 (9th Cir. 2001) (same); Prudential Ins. Co. of America v. Gibraltar Financial Corp. of

27   California, 694 F.2d 1150, 1156 (9th Cir. 1982) (same). “On the other hand, substantial

28

 1   deficiencies in the design or execution of a survey of individuals is grounds for its complete

 2   exclusion.” In re: Autozone, Inc., 2016 WL 4208200, at *16 (N.D.Cal. 2016) (internal quotations

 3   omitted). To that end, defendants cite several cases excluding survey results where the expert failed

 4   to address non-response bias, along with other significant methodological flaws. See Jimenez v.

 5   Allstate Insurance Company, 2019 WL 13088814, at *18 (C.D.Cal. 2019) (expert’s failure to

 6   persuade the court that survey responses were representative of class “weigh[ed] in favor of

 7   granting” Rule 702 motion); Autozone, 2016 WL 4208200, at *18-20 (granting class decertification

 8   motion where problems with the survey were “fundamental,” including that the expert “did not

 9   adequately account for the possibility of nonresponse bias.”)

10          The Court finds these non-binding cases distinguishable and not persuasive here. In

11   Autozone, the court’s concerns about non-response bias were driven by a particular “type of non-

12   response called selected non-response,” that is, that “some of the persons called actively choose not

13   to respond for particular reasons.” 2016 WL 4208200, at *18. Hundreds of respondents actively

14   refused to participate in the Autozone plaintiffs’ survey, such that “refusals outnumbered surveys

15   responded to by almost two-thirds,” an unexplained red flag that raised concerns of systemic errors.

16   Id. The Autozone court was also concerned with “self-interest bias,” the risk that survey results

17   were biased by the incentive for class members to respond in ways to maximize their monetary

18   award. The survey respondents here, in contrast, are not class members or plaintiffs and lack that

19   incentive. Likewise, Jimenez involved concerns not present here, including that 86% of

20   respondents—who were also potential class members—were aware of the lawsuit, and that the

21   expert appeared to have inappropriately combined responses to several questions in making his

22   tabulations. 2019 WL 13088814, at *23-24.

23          As the Third Edition of the Reference Manual on Scientific Evidence notes, “the key is

24   whether nonresponse is associated with systematic differences in response that cannot be

25   adequately modeled or assessed.” Reference Manual on Scientific Evidence (3d ed.) (2011) at 385.

26   The Court does not view the risks of “systematic differences in response” here as particularly high

27   or concerning. As discussed above, the concerns about self-interest bias are not present. Klein also

13   media, depressed, bullied, or suffered diminished social skills; and (b) relies on unreliable or

14   irrelevant sources such as plaintiffs’ fact sheets, “School Health Assessment and Performance

15   Evaluation” profiles (an assessment tool), and “key informant” interviews with teachers and

16   administrators of each district. As a result, defendants argue, Hoover’s causation opinions are not

17   based on independent data but instead regurgitate plaintiffs’ theory. Defendants analogize Hoover’s

18   “key informant” interviews to Therasense, Inc. v. Becton, Dickinson & Co., which decried that

19   “[o]ne of the worst abuses in civil litigation is the attempted spoon-feeding of client-prepared and

20   lawyer-orchestrated ‘facts’ to a hired expert who then ‘relies’ on the information to express an

21   opinion.” 2008 WL 2323856 (N.D.Cal. 2008).

22          The Court disagrees that Therasense is an apt analogy. Hoover explains that her opinions

23   rest on a multiplicity of inputs, including her own research on school mental health systems, review

24   of the scientific literature, graduate-level training in research and medicine, professional experience

25   as a director of the National Center for School Mental Health, interactions with educational

26   providers, and district-specific data. (See Dkt. No. 2407-82, Yeates Decl., Ex. 80, ¶¶ 24-27.) That

27   her interviews with teachers and administrators also informed Hoover’s understanding of how

28

                                                       12
     Case 4:22-md-03047-YGR           Document 2750          Filed 02/17/26      Page 13 of 18




 1   social media affected the districts is hardly surprising, much less nefarious. Hoover’s key

 2   informant interviews, moreover, describe student compulsive use of social media and resulting

 3   distraction and anxiety—evidence that is also offered by affidavits and deposition testimony of the

 4   school districts’ teachers and administrators. Cf. Therasense, 2008 WL 2323856 at *2. (“Coming

 5   from the expert alone . . . any opinion based on such untested and partisan foundation is not based

 6   on sufficient facts and data within the meaning of Rule 702”) (emphasis supplied). Hoover is not

 7   barred on hearsay grounds, either, from considering those interviews when forming her opinion.

 8   See Fannie Mae v. LaRuffa, 702 F. App'x 505, 507 (9th Cir. 2017) (experts may rely on hearsay for

 9   the basis of their opinions). The Court concludes that Hoover’s specific causation opinions are

10   based on sufficient facts and data for the purposes of Rule 702.

11                  3.      Abatement Plan Not Appropriate Remedy

12          Defendants claim that Hoover’s strategic plan is irrelevant and must be excluded because it

13   cannot support a claim for abatement. The Court agrees that Hoover’s plan cannot be used for

14   abatement purposes; however, because the plan supports future damages, her opinions are

15   admissible for that purpose. See Breathitt MSJ Order at 22-23.
                    4.      No Basis for Proposed Staffing Levels, Training, or Timeframe in
16
                            Abatement Plan
17          Defendants maintain that Dr. Hoover’s proposed strategic plans for each school district are
18   mere ipse dixit, including her recommendations about how many new staff to hire, what levels of
19   training to provide, and the duration of the plan. With respect to the latter, defendants criticize Dr.
20   Hoover’s 15-year proposal given her analogy to the tobacco cases which were also 15 years. They
21   assert she provides no explanation for why 15 years would also be effective here.
22          The Court disagrees. “Expert opinion testimony is relevant if the knowledge underlying it
23   has a valid connection to the pertinent inquiry. And it is reliable if the knowledge underlying it has
24   a reliable basis in the knowledge and experience of the relevant discipline.” Primiano, 598 F.3d at
25   565. Here, Hoover’s strategic plan is validly connected to the task at hand—that is, her plan
26   describes a series of interventions focused on student mental health and well-being in relation to
27   social media. (See Dkt. No. 2407-82, Yeates Decl., Ex. 80 ¶¶ 23-36). Hoover applied her
28

                                                        13
     Case 4:22-md-03047-YGR           Document 2750         Filed 02/17/26     Page 14 of 18




 1   knowledge and experience of the relevant discipline, through repeated citation to scientific

 2   literature and drawing upon her professional qualifications as a licensed psychologist and director

 3   of a center for school mental health policy. She hinges the 15 years on the effective lifecycle of a

 4   student’s full school experience as well as with reference to “evidence from large-scale education

 5   reform and mental health implementation science [which] indicates that sustainable system change

 6   takes 10-15 years.” (Hoover Rep. ¶ 112.) Defendants’ disagreement with this metric concerns the

 7   weight of her opinion and does not bar admissibility. See Primiano, 598 F.3d at 564 (“Shaky but

 8   admissible evidence is to be attacked by cross examination, contrary evidence, and attention to the

 9   burden of proof, not exclusion.”).

10              E. DOUGLAS LESLIE

11          Dr. Leslie describes his assignment as one to: “calculat[e] the present value of future costs

12   associated with implementation of the plan . . . recommended by Dr. [Sharon] Hoover” for each

13   school district. (Dkt. No. 2407-14, Yeates Decl., Ex. 96 ¶ 12.) Leslie received a Bachelor of

14   Science degree from the University of Virginia in 1990 and Ph.D. in Economics from Yale

16   over 15 years and holds appointments in the Department of Public Health Sciences and Department

17   of Psychiatry & Behavioral Health at the Penn State College of Medicine. (Id.) Leslie has

18   published hundreds of articles and sits on the editorial board of, among other academic journals,

19   the Journal of Mental Health Policy and Economics. (Id. ¶ 5.) Dr. Leslie’s opinion is limited to

20   costing out Dr. Hoover’s strategic plan.

21          Defendants argue that Hoover’s opinions should be excluded, which in turn requires

22   Leslie’s opinion to be excluded as well. As noted above, however, Hoover’s plan is admissible

23   under Rule 702. In general, “[a]n expert is permitted to rely on the opinion of another expert” and

24   is not required to independently validate it. Int’l Swimming League, 2025 WL 3257200 at *8.

25   Defendants may raise criticisms of Hoover’s plans, and Leslie’s calculations of those plans’ costs,

26   upon cross-examination. In re Apple iPhone Antitrust Litig., 2024 WL 5701895 at *11-13

 1   (N.D.Cal. 2024) (“Objections to an expert’s inputs may be raised on cross-examination.”) The

 3              F. BRIAN OSBORNE

 4          Uniquely among the SD experts, Dr. Osborne offers an opinion based on his “experience in

 8   Ex. 108 ¶¶ 2, 20.) Osborne holds an Ed.D. in Administration, Planning, and Social Policy from the

 9   Harvard Graduate School of Education and an M.A. in Administration, Planning, and Social Policy

10   from Harvard University. (Id. ¶¶ 11, 12.) Osborne has over 25 years of experience in public

11   education, including as a teacher and superintendent of schools in New York and New Jersey. (Id.

12   ¶ 6.) Osborne currently instructs and consults with educators across New Jersey and Pennsylvania,

13   spending approximately 40-50 days a year in schools observing classroom instruction. (Id. ¶ 13.)

14          The Court addresses defendants’ five challenges.

15                  1.      Failure to Account for This Court’s MTD Order

16          As with the other expert opinions, defendants object that Osborne fails to offer an opinion

17   as to defendants’ specific platforms or the actionable features and opines only on social media in

18   general. For the same reasons previously articulated, the objection is overruled.
                    2.      Opinions Lack Reliable Methodology or are Hearsay
19
            Defendants argue Osborne’s lack of a reliable methodology precludes admission of his
20
     opinions. The objection is meritless in this context.
21
            Rule 702 does not bar opinions based on significant experience in a particular industry, nor
22
     does it mandate a scientific analysis in the appropriate circumstance. See Rogers v. Raymark
23
     Indus., Inc., 922 F.2d 1426, 1429 (9th Cir. 1991) (“A witness can qualify as an expert through
24
     practical experience in a particular field, not just through academic training”). Osborne’s opinions
25
     are based not only on discussions with teachers or administrators from plaintiffs’ school districts
26
     but from his considerable work in the industry including his understanding of national trends
27

28

                                                       15
     Case 4:22-md-03047-YGR           Document 2750         Filed 02/17/26      Page 16 of 18




 1   regarding social media in schools. That some of this may be hearsay is not a bar to his opinions,

 2   although it would be a bar if he attempted to regurgitate the hearsay at trial. Supra Section II. As

 3   such, Osborne is not required to exclude from consideration, in forming his opinions, conversations

 4   he has had with other educators throughout his years of practice. That Osborne’s opinions are not

 5   based on interviews or data from the six bellwether plaintiff school districts does not render them

 6   inadmissible, either. Id. at 1430 (“Nor is the expert required to be personally familiar with the facts

 7   or data of the particular case about which he is testifying; an expert opinion may be based on any

 8   type of evidence commonly used by experts in the field.”) As a result, his opinions are not barred

 9   based on a lack of reliable methodology or on hearsay grounds.

10                  3.      AI-Generated Citations

11          Defendants contend that Osborne admitted at deposition that articles upon which he

12   purportedly relied do not exist, and that other incorrect citations were miscites generated by AI.

13   Plaintiffs respond that none of the academic articles were actually false, but rather that citations

14   were merely incorrectly formatted based on use of an AI citation tool, which was subsequently

15   corrected. The Court declines to exclude Osborne’s opinions on this basis. Defendants may,

16   however, raise the issue on cross-examination.
                    4.      Not Qualified to Offer Opinions about Platform Design and Scientific
17
                            Literature on Social Media
18          Defendants maintain that Osborne’s conclusions that social media has adverse mental
19   health effects on youth, and that platforms are engineered to maximize attention, are outside of his
20   expertise as a former school administrator. The opinions that Dr. Osborne offers at a general level,
21   however—that student use of social media disrupts and stresses school operations—do not fall
22   outside of his expertise in school administration. (See Dkt. No. 2407-98, Yeates Decl., Ex. 96 ¶ 4(i-
23   v).) Defendants did not bring a motion to strike. The motion to exclude the entirety of the opinion
24   on these grounds is denied.
25          That said, for the parties’ edification, portions of Osborne’s report do stray from his
26   expertise in school administration into the mental state of students and the design of platforms,
27   topics for which he lacks specialized knowledge. The Court discusses these below.

 1

                    6.      Trial Testimony
 8
            Despite the foregoing rulings, the Court is concerned with a number of the statements made
 9
     in Dr. Osborne’s report. To guide the parties, the Court discusses illustrative but non-exhaustive
10
     examples of several categories of testimony that would be excluded.
11
            One, apropos of the last issue, Osborne may not opine on issues of law or ultimate fact.
12
     Osborne’s report repeatedly describes education as a “public right.” See, e.g., id. at 102. It is not
13
     clear what he means but his claim appears to conflict with controlling precedent. In San Antonio
14
     Independent School Dist. v. Rodriguez, the Supreme Court concluded that education was not a
15
     fundamental right protected by the Constitution. 93 S.Ct. 1278, 1281 (1973). To the extent
16
     Osborne’s use of “public right” is an attempt to wade into the elements of plaintiffs’ public
17
     nuisance claims, he cannot opine on the law. Fed.R.Evid. 702. This will be excluded if proffered in
18
     the manner identified in the report.
19
            Two, portions of Osborne’s report contain statements that are mere argument and not based
20
     on specialized expertise. (See, e.g., Yeates Decl., Ex. 96 ¶¶ 23, 25, 27-32.) For example, at
21
     paragraph 23, Osborne states:
22
                    Indeed, perhaps the only force in young people’s lives that has become
23                  as universal as education is social media. Unfortunately, social media
                    functions not to help educate all children but instead to profit from
24                  exploiting students’ attention, disrupting the operations and
                    educational mission of school districts and their schools in the process.
25
     Statements such as this attempt to advance a narrative rather than offer expertise to a jury and are
26
     not proper grounds for expert testimony. Fed.R.Evid. 702. Testimony to this effect will be
27
     excluded if proffered at trial in the manner identified in the report. Other portions of Osborne’s
28

 1   report likewise contain statements that stray far from expertise and into argument. Id. ¶¶ 88, 89, 90,

 2   101, 103, 107, 108, 110.

 3            Three, and to be explicit, an expert can rely on hearsay to form his opinion but may not

 4   regurgitate it. For example, Osborne repeats information and quotes from transcripts of depositions

 5   of several school administrators in this litigation. (Yeates Decl., Ex. 96 ¶¶ 56-60, 62, 71, 80.) At

 6   trial, Osborne may be permitted to opine that factual information which is independently offered

 7   into evidence is consistent with or supports his opinions. He cannot, however, simply restate

 8   portions of deposition transcripts as fact. Fed.R.Evid. 703. Similarly, Osborne cannot serve as a

 9   conduit for facts and data from external studies and reports, as several portions of his report

10   attempt to do. (See, e.g., id. ¶¶ 61 (describing news stories about viral challenges), 69 (relaying the

11   results of academic studies about social media), 72 (same).)

12            Four, an expert’s testimony must also be reasonably based in his field of knowledge.

13   Fed.R.Evid. 703. Osborne states that students “fear being recorded, mocked, or turned into a

14   meme,” but is not a psychologist nor claims unique insight into the minds and thought processes of

15   students. Id. ¶ 79. Because he is not able to testify to those students’ motivations, such statements

16   could not be offered as testimony.
