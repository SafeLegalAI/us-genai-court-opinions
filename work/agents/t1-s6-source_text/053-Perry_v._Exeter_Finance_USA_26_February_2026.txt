 8                                         DISCUSSION
 9   I.     Legal Standard
10          The Federal Arbitration (“FAA”) applies to contracts “evidencing a transaction
11   involving commerce.” 9 U.S.C. § 2.3 Under the FAA, written agreements to arbitrate
12   disputes “shall be valid, irrevocable, and enforceable, save upon such grounds as exist at
13   law or in equity for the revocation of any contract.” Id. Thus, absent a valid contractual
14   defense, the FAA “leaves no place for the exercise of discretion by a district court, but
15   instead mandates that district courts shall direct the parties to proceed to arbitration on
16   issues as to which an arbitration agreement has been signed.” Dean Witter Reynolds, Inc.,
17   v. Byrd, 470 U.S. 213, 218 (1985).
18          In general, a district court’s role under the FAA is “limited to determining (1)
19   whether a valid agreement to arbitrate exists and, if it does, (2) whether the agreement
20   encompasses the dispute at issue.” Chiron Corp. v. Ortho Diagnostic Sys., Inc., 207 F.3d
21   1126, 1130 (9th Cir. 2000). These two issues are sometimes referred to as the “gateway”
22   questions of arbitrability. Rent-A-Center, W., Inc. v. Jackson, 561 U.S. 63, 68-69 (2010).
23          As the parties seeking to compel arbitration, Defendants bear the burden of proof.
24   Ashbey v. Archstone Prop. Mgmt., Inc., 785 F.3d 1320, 1323 (9th Cir. 2015). “It is
25   permissible to consider evidence outside the pleadings when resolving a motion to compel

 9   II.    The Parties’ Arguments
10          Defendants “move to compel arbitration of [Plaintiff’s] claims and stay the litigation
11   pending completion of the arbitration.” (Doc. 19 at 1.) First, Defendants argue that
12   although the two gateway questions are normally “answered by the Court,” “where, as here,
13   an arbitration agreement expressly delegates to an arbitrator the decision concerning the
14   enforceability and scope of the arbitration agreement, the arbitrator—not the court—has
15   exclusive authority to determine these issues.” (Id. at 7.) Defendants argue that “[h]ere,
16   the Arbitration Provision in the RISC contains a clear and unmistakable delegation clause,
17   stating that the arbitrator, and not a court, must resolve any dispute concerning ‘the
18   interpretation and scope of this Arbitration Provision, and the arbitrability of the claim or
19   dispute.’” (Id. at 8.) Defendants also argue, in the alternative, that “even absent the
20   delegation clause, the result is the same: Plaintiff is required to arbitrate her claims against
21   Defendants.” (Id.) Specifically, Defendants argue that “it is undeniable that Plaintiff
22   expressly agreed to arbitrate her claims with Defendants” because “the Arbitration
23   Provision is highlighted with a text box, bolding and all caps on a page that Plaintiff
24   expressly signed,” “[t]he Arbitration Provision is further referenced on the first and last
25   pages of the RISC,” and “the RISC title announces that it is a contract ‘with Arbitration
26   Provision.’” (Id. at 8-9.) Defendants also argue that “Plaintiff reaffirmed the entire
27   RISC—including her agreement to arbitrate—when she entered into the Settlement

16    III.   Analysis
17           A.     Contract Formation And Delegation Clause
18           As noted, a district court’s role under the FAA is generally “limited to determining
19    (1) whether a valid agreement to arbitrate exists and, if it does, (2) whether the agreement
20    encompasses the dispute at issue.” Chiron Corp., 207 F.3d at 1130. Furthermore,
21    “[a]lthough these questions are ordinarily resolved by the court, parties may agree to
22    arbitrate one or both of the gateway issues by including a delegation clause in the
23    arbitration agreement.” Williams v. Experian Info. Sols. Inc., 2024 WL 3876171, *11 (D.
24    Ariz. 2024). “An agreement to arbitrate a gateway issue is simply an additional, antecedent
25    agreement the party seeking arbitration asks the federal court to enforce, and the FAA
26    operates on this additional arbitration agreement just as it does on any other.” Rent-A-
27    Center, 561 U.S. at 70. The evidence of the parties’ intent to delegate such issues must be
28    “clear and unmistakable.” Brennan v. Opus Bank, 796 F.3d 1125, 1130 (9th Cir. 2015).


                                                 - 12 -
     Case 2:25-cv-01552-DWL        Document 24      Filed 02/26/26     Page 13 of 28



 1           Even if an arbitration provision includes a clause delegating gateway issues to the
 2    arbitrator, certain issues remain non-delegable and must be decided by the court. Williams,
 3    2024 WL 3876171 at *11. For example, “contract-formation issues are always matters for
 4    judicial resolution . . . even in the presence of a delegation clause.” Caremark, LLC v.
 5    Chickasaw Nation, 43 F.4th 1021, 1030 (9th Cir. 2022). The Ninth Circuit has held that
 6    there are three guiding “principles” to assist in determining who—the district court or the
 7    arbitrator—should decide certain issues:
 8           First, a court must resolve any challenge that an agreement to arbitrate was
             never formed, even in the presence of a delegation clause. Next, a court must
 9
             also resolve any challenge directed specifically to the enforceability of the
10           delegation clause before compelling arbitration of any remaining gateway
             issues of arbitrability. Finally, if the parties did form an agreement to
11           arbitrate containing an enforceable delegation clause, all arguments going to
12           the scope or enforceability of the arbitration provision are for the arbitrator
             to decide in the first instance.

12    subcontract.” Id. The same is true here—§ I.A.iii of the Settlement Agreement clearly and

15           Caremark’s second principle is that “a court must also resolve any challenge
16    directed specifically to the enforceability of the delegation clause before compelling

18    As noted, the evidence of the parties’ intent to delegate gateway issues of arbitrability must
19    be “clear and unmistakable.” Brennan, 796 F.3d at 1130.

21    lacks a delegation clause. (See, e.g., Doc. 21 at 2, emphasis added [“Where—as here—

25    In fact, the clause never once mentions the words ‘delegate,’ ‘delegation,’ ‘exclusive
26    authority,’ or anything resembling language that would shift gateway decisions to an
27    arbitrator.”]; id. at 7 [“Even if a delegation clause existed (it does not) . . . .”].) Any such
28    argument is without merit. By expressly agreeing that “the interpretation and scope of this

 1    Arbitration Provision, and the arbitrability of the claim or dispute . . . shall . . . be resolved

 3    “clear and unmistakable” evidence of their intent to arbitrate arbitrability. See, e.g., Adams
 4    v. Conn Appliances Inc., 2017 WL 3315204, *2-3 (D. Ariz. 2017) (where arbitration
 5    provision explicitly provided that it “[c]overs any dispute concerning the arbitrability of
 6    any such controversy or claim,” holding that “[t]he Supreme Court and this Circuit
 7    repeatedly have concluded that similar delegation language is sufficiently clear and

 5    arbitration, this Arbitration Provision shall not apply to such claim or dispute.” (Id.) But
 6    whether Plaintiff’s claims fall within this carve-out is a delegated question regarding the
 7    scope of the Arbitration Provision. Oracle Am., Inc. v. Myriad Grp. A.G., 724 F.3d 1069,
 8    1076 (9th Cir. 2013). Put another way, whether federal law provides that some or all of
 9    Plaintiff’s claims are not subject to binding arbitration is an “arbitrability determination,
10    which the parties have clearly and unmistakably delegated to the arbitrator.” Id.
11           Likewise, Plaintiff argues that her claims “arise[] from conduct that occurred
12    entirely after execution of the Settlement Agreement and in violation of its terms.” (Doc.
13    21 at 10.) But to the extent Plaintiff is attempting to argue that her claims are not subject
14    to arbitration because they occurred post-settlement, that question, again, is one of scope
15    that has been delegated to the arbitrator.
16           Plaintiff also argues at length that Defendants did not demand, and she did not
17    refuse, arbitration, and she contends that § 4 of the FAA requires such a demand and refusal
18    as a condition precedent to arbitration. (Doc. 21 at 2-4.) But even assuming for the sake
19    of argument that Plaintiff’s understanding of § 4 is correct, the question of Defendants’
20    satisfaction of this condition precedent to arbitrability would be a question for the arbitrator
21    to decide. Cf. Quantum Fluids LLC v. Kleen Concepts LLC, 2021 WL 242104, *7 (D.
22    Ariz. 2021) (“The Court need not decide whether Kleen satisfied, or should be excused
23    from satisfying, all of the ADR Clause’s prerequisites to arbitration (i.e., engaging in at
24    least three negotiation sessions and then engaging in mediation) because that issue has been
25    delegated to the arbitrator to decide in the first instance.”). Indeed, even if the Arbitration
26    Provision did not have an enforceable delegation clause, the satisfaction of a condition
27    precedent to arbitrability would still be presumptively a question for the arbitrator. BG
28    Grp., PLC v. Republic of Argentina, 572 U.S. 25, 34-35 (2014) (“[C]ourts presume that the


                                                   - 24 -
     Case 2:25-cv-01552-DWL         Document 24        Filed 02/26/26     Page 25 of 28



 1    parties intend arbitrators, not courts, to decide disputes about the meaning and application
 2    of particular procedural preconditions for the use of arbitration. . . . And they include the
 3    satisfaction of ‘prerequisites such as time limits, notice, laches, estoppel, and other
 4    conditions precedent to an obligation to arbitrate.’”) (cleaned up).
 5           B.      Waiver
 6           As noted, Plaintiff argues that Defendants waived their right to arbitrate through
 7    their pre- and post-litigation conduct. (Doc. 21 at 11-13.) Specifically, Plaintiff argues
 8    that Defendants “were served by the U.S. Marshal and waited more than 60 days—without
 9    raising arbitration as an affirmative defense, without responding to [Plaintiff’s] pre-
10    litigation demands, and without initiating any arbitral forum—before filing this Motion.”
11    (Id. at 15-16.)
12           The first question that must be addressed is who decides whether Defendants have
13    waived, through their litigation conduct, their right to arbitrate—the Court or the arbitrator?
14    The Ninth Circuit “ha[s] made clear that courts generally decide whether a party has waived
15    his right to arbitration by litigation conduct. If the parties intend that an arbitrator decide
16    that issue under a particular contract, they must place clear and unmistakable language to
17    that effect in the agreement.” Martin v. Yasuda, 829 F.3d 1118, 1124 (9th Cir. 2016). As
18    discussed, the Arbitration Provision provides that “[a]ny claim or dispute, . . . including
19    the interpretation and scope of this Arbitration Provision, and the arbitrability of the claim
20    or dispute . . . shall . . . be resolved by neutral, binding arbitration.” (Doc. 20 at 8, emphasis
21    added.) The Court concludes that this language does not provide clear and unmistakable
22    evidence, sufficient to overcome the presumption of judicial resolution, of the parties’
23    intent to have an arbitrator decide issues of waiver by litigation conduct. See, e.g.,
24    Mohamed v. Uber Techs., Inc., 848 F.3d 1201, 1209 & n.4 (9th Cir. 2016) (even where the
25    “Agreements delegated to the arbitrators the authority to decide issues relating to the
26    ‘enforceability, revocability or validity of the Arbitration Provision or any portion of the
27    Arbitration Provision,’” “[t]he arbitration agreements may not have clearly and
28    unmistakably delegated to the arbitrator the authority to decide the question of waiver by


                                                    - 25 -
     Case 2:25-cv-01552-DWL         Document 24       Filed 02/26/26      Page 26 of 28



 1    litigation conduct”); Morgan Stanley & Co. LLC v. Couch, 659 F. App’x 402, 404-05 (9th
 2    Cir. 2016) (concluding, where the arbitration clause provided that “any dispute as to the
 3    arbitrability of a particular issue or claim pursuant to this arbitration provision is to be
 4    resolved in arbitration,” that “[t]his language, requiring that the arbitrability of individual
 5    issues or claims be resolved by the arbitrator, does not encompass disputes over whether
 6    the clause remains valid in light of the parties’ litigation conduct” and that “[t]he arbitration
 7    clause is not clear and unmistakable evidence that the parties intended for an arbitrator to
 8    decide claims of waiver by litigation”); In re Chrysler Pacifica Fire Recall Prods. Liab.
 9    Litig., 143 F.4th 718, 723 (6th Cir. 2025) (“Here, although the clauses at issue broadly
10    delegate to arbitration all disputes over the sales contracts’ ‘validity,’ ‘enforceability,’
11    ‘scope,’ and ‘arbitrability,’ they do not cover gateway questions such as waiver through
12    inconsistent litigation conduct. . . . [A]lthough the clauses do mention questions of
13    ‘arbitrability,’ we believe that this lone reference falls well short of the clear and
14    unmistakable language needed for FCA to overcome the presumption of judicial
15    resolution.”) (citations omitted); Marie v. Allied Home Mortg. Corp., 402 F.3d 1, 15 (1st
16    Cir. 2005) (concluding, where the arbitration agreement provided for the submission of
17    claims and disputes to arbitration “including the arbitrability of any such controversy or
18    claim,” that the use of the term “arbitrability” did not “evince[] a clear and unmistakable
19    intent to have waiver issues decided by the arbitrator” and thus “the question of waiver of
20    Allied’s right to arbitrate due to its participation in EEOC proceedings is properly for the
21    judge”).
22           Even so, Plaintiff’s waiver argument fails on the merits. “A determination of
23    whether the right to compel arbitration has been waived must be conducted in light of the
24    strong federal policy favoring enforcement of arbitration agreements.” Martin, 829 F.3d
25    at 1124 (citation omitted). “Because waiver of the right to arbitration is disfavored, any
26    party arguing waiver of arbitration bears a heavy burden of proof.” Id. (citation omitted).
27    “[T]he test for waiver of the right to compel arbitration consists of two elements: (1)
28    knowledge of an existing right to compel arbitration; and (2) intentional acts inconsistent


                                                   - 26 -
     Case 2:25-cv-01552-DWL          Document 24        Filed 02/26/26   Page 27 of 28



 1    with that existing right.” Hill v. Xerox Bus. Servs., LLC, 59 F.4th 457, 468 (9th Cir. 2023).
 2           The second element is not satisfied here.          Although Plaintiff is correct that
 3    Defendants’ “first act in this Court was to file a Motion to Compel Arbitration” (Doc. 21
 4    at 12), this does not remotely qualify as action inconsistent with Defendants’ right to
 5    arbitration. “[A] party acts inconsistently with exercising the right to arbitrate when it (1)
 6    makes an intentional decision not to move to compel arbitration and (2) actively litigates
 7    the merits of a case for a prolonged period of time in order to take advantage of being in
 8    court.” Martin v. TEKsystems Mgmt. Inc. (Fn), 2021 WL 2334389, *5 (D. Ariz. 2021)
 9    (quoting Newirth by & through Newirth v. Aegis Senior Communities, LLC, 931 F.3d 935,
10    941 (9th Cir. 2019)). On the same day that Defendants were required to answer the
11    complaint, they moved to compel arbitration.            There was no litigation activity by
12    Defendants beforehand. This is the antithesis of the sort of conduct that might support a
13    finding of waiver. Cf. id. (“Here, American Airlines only filed an answer to Plaintiff’s
14    complaint before filing the instant Motion to Compel Arbitration. Although the answer
15    failed to assert the affirmative defense of arbitration, American has not actively litigated
16    the merits of the case for a prolonged period of time and in fact has made an intentional
17    decision to move to compel arbitration. Inlight of American’s actions and the strong policy
18    against finding a waiver of arbitration, this Court finds that American has not acted
19    inconsistent with its right to arbitrate.”).
20           As for Plaintiff’s arguments regarding Defendants “ignor[ing] [Plaintiff’s] multiple
21    written demand letters and CFPB complaint” (Doc. 21 at 12), failing to make a pre-
22    litigation demand for arbitration does not amount to waiver. After all, waiver “requires
23    conduct inconsistent with the arbitration remedy,” and passive pre-litigation inaction by a
24    defendant who is merely being threatened with suit is not inconsistent with that remedy.
25    Okabayashi v. Travelers Home & Marine Ins. Co., 2015 WL 6447400, *3 (D. Ariz. 2015).
26    See also Specialty Merch. Corp. v. Navigators Ins. Co., 2012 WL 12892158, *3 (C.D. Cal.
27    2012) (“Plaintiffs nevertheless argue that Defendant had acted inconsistently with its
28    arbitration right by failing to submit the dispute to arbitration during the approximately


                                                     - 27 -
     Case 2:25-cv-01552-DWL         Document 24       Filed 02/26/26      Page 28 of 28



 1    twelve-month period leading up to the filing of this action, in which the Parties exchanged
 2    correspondence regarding the denial of Plaintiffs’ claims. This argument is unavailing . . . .
 3    Plaintiffs offer no authority to support the proposition that a party’s pre-litigation failure to
 4    invoke the right to arbitration alone could constitute a waiver.”).10
 5           C.     Stay
 6           Defendants ask the Court to stay this action pending completion of the arbitration.
 7    (Doc. 19 at 13.) “When a district court finds that a lawsuit involves an arbitrable dispute,
 8    and a party requests a stay pending arbitration, § 3 of the FAA compels the court to stay
 9    the proceeding.” Smith v. Spizzirri, 601 U.S. 472, 478 (2024). Accordingly, Defendants’
10    stay request is granted.
