            A.      The Deadlines to File Objections to Discharge and Ability to Extend the

 6
            With respect to the deadline to object to a chapter 7 debtor’s discharge pursuant to 11
 7
     U.S.C. § 727, Rule 4004 states:
 8
 9           (a)    Time to Object to Discharge; Notice.

10           (1) Chapter 7. In a chapter 7 case, a complaint, or a motion under               §
             727(a)(8) or (9) – objecting to a discharge must be filed within 60 days after the
11
             first date set for the § 341(a) meeting of creditors.
12           …
             (b) Extension the Time to File an Objection.
13
             (1) Motion Before the Time Expires. On a party in interest’s motion and after
14
             notice and hearing, the court may, for cause, extend the time to object to a

18

 2          B.      Express, Apparent and Implied Authority to Act on Client’s Behalf
 3
            When a case is in federal court pursuant to federal question jurisdiction, federal common
 4
     law controls an attorney’s authority to enter into stipulations and settlements. Hamilton v.
 5
 6   Willms, No. 1:02-cv-6583 AWI SMS, 2007 WL 2558615, at *7 (E.D. Cal. Sept. 4, 2007); see

 7   also Kelley v. Euromarket Designs, Inc., No. CIVS07-0232 RRB EFB, 2008 WL 109332, at *3
 8
     (E.D. Cal. Jan. 8, 2008).
 9
            Pursuant to federal common law, an attorney’s ability to bind a client to the consequences
10
     of the attorney’s actions is governed by the principals of agency.
11
12          The Restatement of Agency defines agency as "the fiduciary relationship that arises
            when one person (a 'principal') manifests assent to another person (an 'agent') that
13          the agent shall act on the principal's behalf and subject to the principal's control,
            and the agent manifests assent or otherwise consents so to act." Restatement (Third)
14
            of Agency § 1.01 (2006). The legal consequences of an agent's actions may be
15          attributed to a principal when the agent is acting within its authority. Restatement
            (Third) of Agency § 2 intro. note (2006).
16
17   Salyers v. Metro. Life Ins. Co., 871 F.3d 934, 939–40 (9th Cir. 2017).

18          The legal consequences of an agent's actions may be attributed to a principal when the
19   agent has actual authority (express or implied) or apparent authority. Restatement (Third) of
20
     Agency § 2 intro. note (A.L.I. 2006).
21
                    1. Express Authority
22
23          An agent acts with express authority when a principal directly states that the agent may

24   perform a particular act on the principal's behalf. See Salyers v. Metro. Life Ins. Co., 871 F.3d at
25   940 ("Express actual authority derives from an act specifically mentioned to be done in a written
26
     or oral communication.").
27
28

 1                    2. Apparent Authority
 2             As stated in the Restatement (Third) Of Agency § 2.03 (A.L.I. 2006):
 3
               Apparent authority is the power held by an agent or other actor to affect a principal's
 4             legal relations with third parties when a third party reasonably believes the actor
               has authority to act on behalf of the principal and that belief is traceable to the
 5             principal's manifestations.
 6
     Emphasis added. "[I]n order to create apparent authority, the principal must manifest to the third
 7
     party that he 'consents to have the act done on his behalf by the person purporting to act for
 8
 9   him.'" Fennell v. TLB Kent Co., 865 F.2d 498, 502 (2d Cir. 1989) (quoting Restatement (Second)

10   of Agency § 27 (A.L.I. 1958)). Explicit statements made by a principal directly to a third party
11
     and statements made by other parties concerning an agent’s authority that reach the third party
12
     and are traceable to the principal are examples of a "principal’s manifestations" which can
13
     credibly result in the third party believing a purported agent has authority to act on the
14
15   principal’s behalf. Restatement (Third) Of Agency § 2.03 cmt. c (A.L.I. 2006). In some
16   circumstances, the conduct of a principal can be a non-verbal manifestation, such as when a
17
     "principal's acts speak so loudly that explicit verbal communication is unnecessary." Id. § 3.03
18
     cmt. b.

20                     3. Implied Authority

21             Restatement (Third) Of Agency § 2.01 cmt. b (A.L.I. 2006) states:
22             "Implied authority" is often used to mean actual authority either (1) to do what is
23             necessary, usual, and proper to accomplish or perform an agent's express
               responsibilities or (2) to act in a manner in which an agent believes the principal
24             wishes the agent to act based on the agent's reasonable interpretation of the
               principal's manifestation in light of the principal's objectives and other facts known
25             to the agent. These meanings are not mutually exclusive. Both fall within the
26             definition of actual authority.

27             In England v. Doyle, 281 F.2d 304 (9th Cir. 1960), the Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals
28
     noted that “an attorney has implied authority to make agreements and stipulations with respect to

 1   purely procedural matters during the regular course of litigation.” The Court of Appeals further
 2   explained that an attorney “has no such authority to enter into agreements which involve a
 3
     waiver of any of his client's substantial rights or the imposition upon him of new liabilities or
 4
     burdens.” Id. at 308-309 (internal citations omitted); see also Lerner Master Fund, LLC v. Page
 5
 6   (In re Paige), 476 B.R. 867, 870 (Bankr. M.D. Pa. 2012) ("In matters concerning the

 7   management of the litigation, implied authority is initially presumed for the sake of judicial
 8
     efficiency; an attorney may typically enter into procedural stipulations and agreements.").
 9
            In Paige, the debtors disputed that they had provided their attorney with the authority to
10
     make a stipulation to extend the deadline to file a complaint challenging the dischargeability of
11
12   their debt; consequently, the debtors contended that a stipulated extension of time between the

13   parties’ counsel was not binding on them.
14
            The court held that the extension of the objection to discharge deadline “involved mere
15
     procedural issues.” Id., 476 B.R. at 871. As explained by the court:
16
17          [I]t is initially presumed that [the debtors’ counsel] had the implied authority to
            agree to the stipulation. The joint stipulation did not deny the Paiges any substantive
18          rights; it merely dealt with a filing deadline, set, not by statute, but by Federal Rule
            of Bankruptcy Procedure 4007. The joint stipulation did not burden the Paiges in
19          any way, nor did it deprive the Paiges of the dischargeability of their debt.
20
     Id. When the totality of circumstances was weighed, the Paige court held there was not enough
21
     evidence to rebut the presumption of implied authority that generally attaches to procedural
22
23   matters, such as the stipulation to an extension of time by the debtors’ counsel.

                    Procedural
25
26          In In re Hill, 811 F.2d 484 (9th Cir. 1987), the Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals considered

27   whether Rules 4007(c) and 9006(d) abridge affected parties’ substantive rights, as codified in 11
28
     U.S.C. § 523. In Hill, more than 60 days after a debtor’s first meeting of creditors, a creditor

 1   filed a complaint pursuant to § 523(a)(6). On appeal, the creditor argued that the deadline
 2   imposed in Rule 4007(c) is functionally a statute of limitations to bring a complaint to determine

 7          The Court of Appeals rejected the creditor’s argument:
 8
            Although rule 4007(c) in some respects resembles a statute of limitations, its effects
 9          are quite different. Statutes of limitations affect primary conduct—behavior apart
            from any litigation—by providing repose to potential defendants. Such statutes are
10          not designed to manage ongoing litigation. In contrast, rule 4007(c) is triggered
            only by certain events within bankruptcy litigation. The rule does not grant repose
11
            to the debtor or anyone else. Instead, it avoids delay by requiring a party to file
12          promptly rather than just prior to the final settlement of the bankruptcy case. See
            Bankr. R. 9006 advisory committee's note (such rules serve the "interest of prompt
13          administration of bankruptcy cases"). Therefore, it affects procedural not
            substantive rights.
14
15   Id. at 486–87.

 6
              For purposes of subsection (b)(1), parties should be bound by and accountable for

15
     previously called intrinsic or extrinsic) misrepresentation, or misconduct by an opposing party.”
16

18   committed by "an opposing party." Latshaw v. Trainer Wortham & Co., 452 F.3d at 1102; Valley
19   National Bank v. Needler (In re Grantham Bros.), 922 F.2d 1438, 1442–43 (9th Cir.1991)

            A.      Whether the Swift Extension Stipulations Are Binding on Debtor
10
                    1. Actual Express Authority
11
12          Mr. Gorginian has acknowledged that he acted unilaterally in agreeing to provide Swift

13   with the extensions of time to file a complaint against Debtor. Debtor did not explicitly assent
14
     nor instruct Mr. Gorginian to enter into the Swift Extension Stipulations. As a result, Mr.
15
     Gorginian did not possess express authority.
16
17                  2. Apparent Authority

18          Swift argues that, although Mr. Gorginian may not have express authority, he had
19   apparent authority from Debtor, which is sufficient to bind Debtor to the Swift Extension
20
     Stipulations. Mr. Schlecter understood that Mr. Gorginian was acting expressly on Debtor’s
21
     behalf. Consequently, Swift relied on Mr. Gorginian’s consent to the Swift Extension
22
23   Stipulations. Schlecter Decl., ¶ 31-32, 49 and 52.

24          Mr. Schlecter’s reliance on Mr. Gorginian’s acts and representations is not sufficient to
25   establish apparent authority for Mr. Gorginian, acting as an agent, to bind Debtor as a principal.
26
     In order to establish “apparent authority,” Swift must show that Swift relied upon a statement or
27
     other manifestations of Debtor. Swift has not shown that Debtor, or anyone other than Mr.

                    3. Implied Authority
 4

 5
 6   granting Swift’s requested extension of time would enable Debtor to provide Swift with

     from filing a complaint against Debtor.
16
17          Regarding procedural matters during the regular course of litigation, an attorney acts with

18   implied authority and may bind their client through words and actions. See England v. Doyle,
