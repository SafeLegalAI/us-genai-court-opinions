15                                             ANALYSIS
16          Because Hunter is proceeding in forma pauperis, the court must consider whether
17   his complaint states any claims on which he might be able to obtain relief. 28 U.S.C.
18   § 1915(e)(1). This requires the complaint “contain sufficient factual matter, accepted as
19   true, to state a claim to relief that is plausible on its face.” Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556 U.S. 662,
20   678 (2009) (simplified). This is not a “probability requirement,” but a requirement that the
21   factual allegations show “more than a sheer possibility that a defendant has acted
22   unlawfully.” Id. A claim is facially plausible “when the plaintiff pleads factual content that
23   allows the court to draw the reasonable inference that the defendant is liable for the
24   misconduct alleged.” Id. “Determining whether a complaint states a plausible claim for
25   relief . . . [is] a context-specific task that requires the reviewing court to draw on its judicial

 2          Beginning with Hunter’s claims for race and sex discrimination under Title VII, a
 3   prima facie case of such discrimination requires the plaintiff show: (1) he belongs to a class
 4   of persons protected by Title VII; (2) he performed his job satisfactorily; (3) he suffered an
 5   adverse employment action; and (4) his employer treated him differently than a similarly-
 6   situated employee who does not belong to the same protected class.2 See McDonnell
 7   Douglas Corp. v. Green, 411 U.S. 792, 802 (1973). Hunter alleges he belongs to a protected
 8   class, but he does not provide sufficiently clear allegations to establish any of the remaining
 9   requirements. Hunter does not allege he was performing adequately, and he does not clearly
10   identify the adverse employment action he suffered. That is, Hunter may believe the
11   adverse action was the “pattern of harassment” or he may believe it was his termination.
12   He might also be attempting to identify both as actionable. The complaint simply does not
13   make this clear.
14          In addition to failing to identify the adverse action at issue, Hunter also fails to
15   identify any other similarly-situated employee he believes was treated differently. Nor has
16   Hunter alleged any other facts indicating he was treated differently because of his race or
17   sex. Overall, Hunter has not provided sufficient background facts for the court to conclude
18   he may have been the victim of discrimination. His race and sex discrimination claims are
19   dismissed.

22   action; and (3) a causal connection exists between the protected activity and the adverse
23   employment action. Davis v. Team Elec. Co., 520 F.3d 1080, 1093–94 (9th Cir. 2008).
24   Hunter alleges he “complained to management and Human Resources regarding

 3   complaints, Hunter was subject to “harassment, targeting, and retaliatory treatment” but he

 9          Hunter then alleges a claim for wrongful termination. Arizona law “strictly limits
10   the claims available to an employee alleging wrongful termination.” Peterson v. City of
11   Surprise, 418 P.3d 1020, 1025 (Ariz. Ct. App. 2018). Such a claim may be brought only
12   when “(1) a discharge is in violation of an employment contract; (2) a discharge violates
13   an Arizona statute; or (3) a discharge is in retaliation for the employee’s assertion of certain
14   rights protected by state law.” Guernsey v. Elko Wire Rope Inc., No. CV-21-00848-PHX-
15   DJH, 2023 WL 5348567, at *2 (D. Ariz. Aug. 21, 2023). Hunter does not provide factual
16   allegations establishing any of these possibilities. He does not identify any employment
17   contract, he does not identify an Arizona statute violated by his termination, and he does
18   not allege he was terminated in retaliation for complaints regarding violations of state law.
19   The wrongful termination claim is dismissed.
20          Hunter’s final claim is for defamation. Such a claim requires allegations the speaker
21   “publishes a false and defamatory communication” when the speaker “(a) knows that the
22   statement is false and it defames the other, (b) acts in reckless disregard of these matters,
23   or (c) acts negligently in failing to ascertain them.” Dube v. Likins, 167 P.3d 93, 104 (Ariz.
24   Ct. App. 2007) (simplified). Hunter identifies the relevant statement as one that accused
25   him of “theft or criminal conduct.” (Doc. 20 at 1.) But he does not provide any meaningful
26   additional details, such as the identity of the speaker, how the statement was published, or
27   why that speaker knew the statement was false. Perhaps most importantly, Hunter appears
28   to place the statement as occurring prior to 2022. If that is when the statement was made,

 1   any defamation claim would be barred by the one-year statute of limitations. A.R.S.
 2   § 12-541(1). Without adequate factual allegations and likely barred by the statute of
 3   limitations, the current defamation claim is dismissed.
 4            Hunter has not provided sufficient factual allegations to state any claim, but he may
 5   be able to add sufficient additional allegations. Hunter therefore is granted leave to file
 6   one—and only one—amended complaint. If he chooses to file an amended complaint,
 7   Hunter must explain what happened, who was involved, and why he believes certain
 8   actions were based on his race, sex, or previous protected activity. Hunter must also
 9   identify how his termination qualifies as actionable under the limited circumstances
10   Arizona law recognizes for a viable wrongful termination claim. And he must identify
11   when the defamatory statement was made, who made it, and how it was conveyed to a third
12   party.
13            Finally, some of Hunter’s filings suggest he may be using generative artificial
14   intelligence (“generative AI”).3 The court has a general policy that requires any party that
15   uses generative AI in connection with filings to attach to the subject filing a separate
16   declaration disclosing the use of generative AI and certifying that the filer, in the exercise
17   of the filer’s independent judgment, has personally reviewed and verified the content of
18   the filing as accurate and in compliance with Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 11. The
19   declaration must identify which, if any, portion of the filing incorporates Generative AI
20   outputs. Any party who presents to the court a pleading, written motion, or other paper
21   incorporating inaccurate or undeclared generative AI outputs, including but not limited to
22   inaccurate or non-existent case citations, may be subject to sanctions including dismissal
23   without further warning. In preparing his amended complaint, Hunter must comply with
24   the court’s procedures regarding generative AI.
